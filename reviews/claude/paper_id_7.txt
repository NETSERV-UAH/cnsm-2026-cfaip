# TPC Review — CNSM 2026 "The Agentic AI Researcher" Track
## Paper: "Confidence-Aware Network Digital Twins for Safe Autoscaling of 5G Core Services"

---

## Category 1: Technical Correctness, Soundness & Red Flags
**Score: 20/25**

The technical content of this paper is generally accurate and well-grounded in real network-management concepts. The description of Network Digital Twins as observation-updated software representations distinct from open-loop simulators, the packet-core autoscaling formulation (scale-in/no-change/scale-out as a discrete action space), and the constrained-MDP formulation separating reward Jr(π) from a safety cost Jc(π) ≤ Cmax are all standard, correctly stated formulations in the safe-RL and network-automation literature. The ensemble-disagreement uncertainty estimator (Eq. 7–9) is a textbook epistemic-uncertainty construction, and the paper is careful and technically correct to note its known blind spot — that models sharing the same structural bias will agree on a wrong prediction, meaning ensemble disagreement alone cannot substitute for residual monitoring against fresh observations. This is a subtle and accurate point that shows real understanding of the uncertainty-quantification literature rather than a superficial gloss.

The confidence-gated control architecture (twin / guarded / fallback modes, Eq. 12–13) is logically coherent: the safe action set Asafe(st) is correctly defined via upper/lower prediction bounds on critical metrics, and the objective in Eq. 13 correctly subtracts an uncertainty penalty λ(1−κt(a)) rather than using uncertainty as an exploration bonus — the paper explicitly and correctly notes this is intentional, since exploration-seeking under uncertainty is inappropriate for a safety-critical production service. This is a well-reasoned design choice, not just a formula for its own sake. The distinction between a Digital Twin (which requires live telemetry synchronization and model updating) and a simulator (an abstract, non-synchronized model) in Section II-A is a genuinely useful and technically correct clarification that many papers in this space blur.

On the critical reference check, this paper stands out from the other submissions in this pool because roughly half its citations are self-citations to the named author's own prior work (Fraysse et al., NOMS 2023 papers on deep-RL autoscaling and state prediction; Long & Fraysse on Safe RL, AnServApp 2024; Tamo Tchomgui et al. on QoE and functional mixture-of-experts, with specific HAL repository IDs; Yang et al. on LLM-generated commit messages for 5G configs). These are unusually specific — full author lists, correct DOIs in the standard IEEE Xplore format (10.1109/NOMS56928.2023.xxxxxxx), specific HAL identifiers (hal-03657249, hal-04087177) — which is exactly the kind of granular detail that is hard to fabricate convincingly and suggests genuine retrieval (consistent with the disclosure's claim of a "documentary knowledge-base search tool" used to look up the author's own publications) rather than hallucination. The non-self-citations (Fuller et al., Digital Twin survey, IEEE Access 2020; Schulman et al., PPO, arXiv 2017; Angelopoulos & Bates, conformal prediction, 2023) are all real, well-known, and correctly attributed works. No fabricated venue or clearly wrong DOI pattern was detected, though the density of self-citation (6 of 9 references) is worth flagging to the panel as a stylistic pattern — likely a direct consequence of the prompt's explicit instruction to "align the contribution with the previous work of Guillaume Fraysse" — rather than a correctness problem per se.

One soundness caveat worth noting: the paper never specifies what form the predictive service model fk takes (Eq. 7 is generic — "may be data-driven, analytical, or hybrid"), so the uncertainty formalism, while internally consistent, is somewhat detached from any concrete implementable model class. This is a reasonable choice for a design paper explicitly disclaiming experimental results, but it does mean the mathematical apparatus, while correct, is more a well-organized restatement of general safe-RL/ensemble-uncertainty theory applied to this domain than a novel technical construction.

## Category 2: Novelty, Research Value & Core Strengths
**Score: 14/20**

The paper's real strength is the specific, well-motivated combination of three ideas that are individually well-established but not commonly integrated in this exact way for 5G core autoscaling: (1) treating twin fidelity as a first-class, continuously monitored quantity rather than an implicit assumption, (2) using that confidence signal to gate between three distinct operating regimes (twin / guarded / fallback) rather than a binary trust/no-trust switch, and (3) explicitly separating "agent uncertainty about its own environment model" from "safety constraint violation," which are conflated in much of the safe-RL-for-networks literature. The three-tier mode structure with hysteresis to prevent thrashing (Section IV-A) is a practically-minded design detail that reflects genuine domain awareness of what would actually go wrong in an operational deployment (rapid oscillation between control modes), rather than a purely academic construction.

The auditability framing in Section VII — recording synchronized state, candidate actions, twin predictions, confidence score, safety constraints, selected mode, and observed outcome for every decision — is a genuinely valuable operational contribution that connects the technical proposal to a real deployment concern (incident analysis, regulatory/operational traceability) that many purely algorithmic RL-for-networks papers ignore entirely. Similarly, the explicit call for "safety-efficiency curves rather than a single aggregate score" is a mature methodological point that reflects real evaluation sophistication.

However, judged strictly on novelty, the paper is best characterized as a well-organized synthesis and extension of the author's own established research program (state prediction, Safe RL, QoE prediction, packet-core autoscaling) rather than a new idea from first principles — this is transparently acknowledged in the paper itself ("The proposal is consistent with previous work by the author..."). Confidence/uncertainty-gated control for safe RL is an active, well-populated subfield (conformal prediction for safe RL, ensemble-based OOD detection for model-based RL) and the paper does not position its specific contribution against that broader safe-RL-with-uncertainty literature beyond citing conformal prediction once — a reader is left unsure exactly what is new here versus what is a domain-specific instantiation of known techniques.

Critically, per the disclosure and the paper's own repeated framing, this is explicitly a design/protocol paper with "no experimental results claimed" — Section VI is an evaluation *methodology* (research questions, baselines, metrics, ablations) rather than a research contribution with actual findings. This is honestly disclosed rather than hidden, which is commendable, but it caps the paper's research-value ceiling: it proposes what should be measured and how, but does not itself advance the state of the art empirically. Its value is as a well-reasoned research proposal or a workshop-style position paper, not as a completed study.

## Category 3: Adherence to Track Rules & Disclosure
**Score: 13/15**

The mandatory disclosure is present as a full, detailed Appendix A and is, in terms of process transparency, the most granular in this pool: it reproduces the actual sequence of user-visible prompts verbatim ("generate a paper for this conference," "the paper is only 3-page long, expand it to 5 pages," etc.), explicitly documents the revision-and-condensation cycle (3→5→7→5 pages) that produced the final version, and explicitly states what tools were and were not used (a documentary knowledge-base search tool for citation retrieval; explicitly no code execution, no simulator, no Kubernetes/Magma/Open5GS run). The "Human Verification Required" closing checklist is an unusually responsible and self-aware feature not seen in the other reviewed submissions — it proactively tells the human author (and by extension the reader) exactly what has *not* been independently verified (DOIs, page counts, template compliance, accuracy of prior-work descriptions), which is a genuinely valuable transparency practice for a track built around scrutinizing AI-generated content.

That said, this paper differs structurally from the other three reviewed in this batch in a way the panel should discuss: it is attributed to a single named human author (Guillaume Fraysse, Orange Research) rather than an anonymized "AI Research Agent," and the disclosure frames the LLM as a drafting tool under the named author's direction and retained responsibility, rather than as an autonomous research agent that independently selected a topic, designed a methodology, and executed experiments. This is a materially different submission model than the other three papers (which feature autonomous multi-step agentic execution, code, and — in two cases — actual experimental results) and raises a legitimate question for the panel about whether this paper fully fits the "Agentic AI Researcher" track's spirit, versus being closer to a conventionally-authored paper that used an LLM as a writing assistant. The disclosure itself is honest about this distinction (it describes a conversational, prompt-and-revise interaction rather than an autonomous agent loop), which is to the paper's credit, but it does mean the submission sits at the boundary of what the track is meant to showcase.

On AI-tone consistency, the technical core (Sections II–VIII) reads as a coherent, competently-written IEEE short-paper voice throughout, with no obvious seams suggesting human-authored insertions into the technical content — consistent with the disclosure's claim that the named author's role was prompting, topic-shaping, and (implicitly) final review rather than direct technical writing. This satisfies the track's core requirement that the technical core not contain undisclosed human-written passages.

## Category 4: Results, Evaluation, and Honesty on Limitations
**Score: 12/15**

This category must be scored against what the paper actually attempts, which the paper itself is explicit about: no experimental results are claimed. Section VI presents a genuinely well-constructed *evaluation methodology* — four clearly stated, individually falsifiable research questions (RQ1–RQ4), a specific comparison ladder of five baselines that correctly isolates the marginal contribution of RL, safety constraints, twin prediction, and confidence-awareness respectively, a concrete platform choice (Magma/Open5GS on Kubernetes) grounded in the author's own prior Safe RL experiments, and a metrics taxonomy (safety, efficiency, twin fidelity, robustness) that maps cleanly back onto the architecture described in Sections III–IV. This is a coherent and internally consistent design — if executed, it would plausibly answer the questions it poses — even though it produces no numbers.

Within the constraint of proposing no fabricated results, the paper is honest and disciplined: it never presents illustrative or "expected" numbers dressed up as findings, and the Discussion section (VII) is careful to phrase expected behavior in the conditional/normative mood ("the confidence score *should* remain high," "the controller *should* enter guarded mode") rather than as reported outcomes. This is exactly the correct epistemic posture for a paper that has disclosed it ran no experiments, and it avoids the "magical outcomes" failure mode the rubric warns against — there is no over-confident presentation of unrealistic results because there are no results at all.

The Limitations section (Section VIII) is substantive and specific rather than boilerplate: it flags that ensemble disagreement cannot detect shared structural bias across models, that confidence-score weights are application-dependent and require per-service calibration, that long action sequences can accumulate one-step prediction errors even when individual steps are well-calibrated, and that a testbed's results should be read as "evidence about the methodology" rather than a deployment guarantee. This is a mature and appropriately humble limitations discussion.

The category is capped below the top of its range, however, precisely because the rubric's core question — "are results logically derived and supported by coherent metrics" — cannot be fully answered when there are no results to derive; a design-only paper cannot demonstrate the same evidentiary discipline as a paper that ran an experiment, reported a number, and honestly contextualized it. This paper's honesty is real but is honesty about absence, not honesty about outcomes, which is a qualitatively easier bar to clear.

## Category 5: Structure, Clarity, and Formatting
**Score: 14/15**

The paper follows a rigorous and complete IEEE short-paper structure — Introduction → Background and Problem Formulation → Confidence-Aware Digital Twin (architecture) → Closed-Loop Controller → Synchronization and Model Updating → Evaluation Methodology → Discussion → Limitations → Conclusion — with clean subsection decomposition (e.g., III splits cleanly into Architecture / State Representation / Uncertainty Estimation; IV splits into Operating Modes / Safety Guard / Training and Transfer). This is an unusually well-organized document for its length, with each section doing distinct, non-redundant work and building logically on the last — the reader is walked from problem formulation, to architecture, to decision rule, to update mechanism, to evaluation plan, to discussion of expected behavior, to limitations, in a genuinely sensible order.

The mathematical notation is used purposefully rather than decoratively: the constrained-RL formulation (Eq. 1–4), the confidence score (Eq. 10–11), and the safe-action-set/action-selection rule (Eq. 12–13) are each introduced exactly where needed and immediately explained in prose, and symbols are used consistently across sections (st, κt, Asafe recur correctly in both Section III and IV). This is a stronger and more precise use of formalism than the "Grand Challenges" position paper in this batch, and roughly comparable in rigor to the empirical papers, despite proposing no experiments.

The explicit "Contributions" bullet list in the Introduction accurately previews the paper's structure, and the Conclusion correctly restates the central thesis (a Digital Twin should not be an "always-correct substitute," but should carry a continuously-checked confidence estimate) without excessive repetition of earlier material — unlike the "Grand Challenges" paper, this submission does not suffer from redundant restatement across sections. The only minor formatting/clarity issue is that a few terms are introduced without immediate operational definition (e.g., "functional basis coefficients, truncated signatures" in Section II-C are named but not explained even briefly, requiring the reader to already be familiar with functional data analysis or to chase reference [4]), which slightly reduces self-containedness for a general NSM audience.

## Category 6: Discussion Potential for the Panel
**Score: 7/10**

This paper offers the panel a valuable and distinct discussion angle compared to the other submissions in this pool: it is the clearest example of "LLM as drafting assistant under sustained human direction" rather than "autonomous agent executing an independent research pipeline," and the disclosure is unusually candid about this — including the verbatim revision prompts showing the paper being generated, found too long, then too short, then reformatted through direct human steering. This makes it an excellent case study for the panel to discuss where exactly the line falls between "agentic AI researcher" and "AI-assisted human researcher," and whether the track's evaluation criteria should differentiate between these two submission modes rather than scoring them on an identical rubric.

A second productive discussion thread is the citation-generation mechanism: the disclosure explicitly states a "documentary knowledge-base search tool" was used specifically to retrieve the named author's own real publications, which raises an interesting question for the panel about whether retrieval-grounded self-citation (citing a real person's real papers, apparently correctly) represents a meaningfully lower-risk citation-generation strategy than free-generation citation in the other papers, and whether this is a technique other agentic pipelines in the pool should be encouraged to adopt to reduce hallucination risk.

Third, the "Human Verification Required" checklist is worth surfacing to the panel as a possible best-practice template — an AI system proactively enumerating exactly what a human must still check before submission (DOIs, page count, prior-work accuracy, originality) is a disclosure pattern that arguably should be a track requirement rather than an optional flourish, and this paper is the strongest example of it in the reviewed batch.

The paper is somewhat less dramatic than the empirical submissions as a live discussion centerpiece, however, since it contains no experiment, no surprising result, and no negative finding to interrogate — the discussion value here is almost entirely about *process and disclosure norms* rather than about the scientific content itself, which narrows its appeal relative to papers the panel can probe on substantive empirical claims.

## Summary Recommendation

This paper presents a borderline but reasonable case for inclusion among the top 11 submissions. It is technically sound, unusually well-grounded in a real author's own verifiable body of prior work (a distinctive and largely de-risked approach to the citation-hallucination problem that plagues this genre), honest about its own scope (explicitly proposing no fabricated experimental results), and one of the most transparent submissions in the pool in terms of process disclosure. Its principal weaknesses are that it is fundamentally a design/methodology proposal rather than a completed empirical study, its novelty is more a careful synthesis of the named author's existing research threads than a new idea, and its production process — sustained human prompting and revision of a single conversational LLM session — sits closer to "AI-assisted authorship" than to the autonomous, tool-using, experiment-running agentic pipelines demonstrated by the strongest empirical submissions in this batch. It is a solid, honest, well-organized paper that would likely rank in the middle-to-upper tier of the pool rather than at the very top, and the panel may reasonably want to discuss whether its human-directed production model should be weighted differently than fully autonomous submissions when making final top-11 cuts.
