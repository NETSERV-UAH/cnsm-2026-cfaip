# TPC Review — CNSM 2026 "The Agentic AI Researcher" Track
## Paper: "Server-to-Edge Ranking Transfer for Telecom LLMs"

---

## Category 1: Technical Correctness, Soundness & Red Flags
**Score: 23/25**

This paper demonstrates real, hands-on hardware and systems expertise rather than a superficial gloss of edge-deployment concepts. The experimental setup is described with genuine technical specificity: a Jetson AGX Orin at named power modes (15W/30W/MAXN), llama.cpp pinned to an exact commit (22dc605c) built for CUDA sm_87, model files identified by SHA-256 hashes (with the multipart-file caveat about hashing only the first shard correctly noted), verified peak temperatures below throttling thresholds, and INA3221 power-rail sampling at 20Hz across three distinct rails (GPU/SOC, CPU/CV, 5V system input). This is the level of concrete, verifiable hardware detail that gives real confidence the measurements are genuine rather than simulated or estimated — a meaningfully different evidentiary posture than papers relying on synthetic or purely simulated results.

The energy-accounting methodology is technically sophisticated and correctly distinguishes two legitimately different, non-interchangeable quantities: gross energy (trapezoidal integral of total rail power, relevant when "a battery powers the whole board") versus net energy (subtracting idle baseline, relevant for "an always-on device"). The paper doesn't just define these two metrics; it uses the distinction to derive a genuinely non-obvious, technically correct operational conclusion — that the *optimal power mode itself flips* depending on which accounting convention is used (MAXN is best under gross accounting, 30W best under net) — which is a sound and important observation about a real methodological ambiguity in edge-energy benchmarking that many papers in this space overlook entirely.

The paper's core empirical claims about quantization behavior (Q4 typically slower than Q8 due to "long-prompt prefill and dequantization overhead," correctly distinguishing this from Q4's genuine memory savings) are technically plausible and consistent with known Jetson/GGU behavior, and the paper is appropriately careful about causal attribution where the evidence doesn't support a strong claim: the Llama-3.2-1B "runtime collapse" (answering "A" on 99.9–100% of questions across four different precisions) is reported with an explicit and correct epistemic caveat — "although the failure is associated with the artifact–runtime combination, all four precisions use the same runtime, so we cannot isolate the cause; this is a reproducible association rather than a proven mechanism." This is precise, correctly-scoped causal language, distinguishing association from mechanism exactly where the experimental design cannot support a stronger claim.

The reference list is dense with very recent (2024–2026) arXiv preprints specific to edge-LLM and telecom-LLM benchmarking, and while this reviewer cannot independently verify the newest entries, the pattern is highly consistent with genuine, careful sourcing: precise, specific arXiv identifiers, correct author-topic pairing (Ferrag/Lakas/Debbah's 6G-Bench and related tiny-model papers are cited consistently and specifically across three separate references), a correctly identified DOI-bearing published version of TeleQnA (IEEE Network 2026) alongside its original arXiv preprint (2310.15051, correctly dated 2023) rather than conflating the two, and a NetSoft 2026 DOI for the Sguotti et al. paper. No fabrication pattern (wrong venue-year pairing, suspiciously round page numbers, mismatched authorship) is evident, and the paper's explicit disclosure of a self-caught v1 scoring bug (the lenient TeleQnA parser crediting echoed option digits, corrected in v1→final with the exact affected score noted: 0.038→0.000 for SmolLM2-135M) is a concrete, checkable instance of genuine methodological self-correction rather than a merely claimed one.

## Category 2: Novelty, Research Value & Core Strengths
**Score: 18/20**

This paper's core contribution is precise, well-motivated, and genuinely underexplored: virtually all telecom-LLM benchmarking work evaluates models on data-center GPUs (A100s, L40S, RTX A6000), yet the actual deployment target for many envisioned 5G/6G agentic use cases is power- and memory-constrained edge hardware, and the paper's central empirical question — do server-derived rankings actually transfer to the edge, including the *identity of the best model* — is exactly the right question to ask and had not, per the paper's stated 138-work survey, been previously answered with both telecom-task accuracy and energy-per-query measured together on a real power-constrained device. This is a genuinely novel and practically important gap, and the paper's finding directly and substantively fills it.

The paper's single most valuable result is the discovery that a mixture-of-experts model (LFM2-8B-A1B) absent from the source paper's own published ranking beats the server-declared winner (Qwen2.5-7B) by a statistically significant 4.0 points while using roughly half the energy and running twice as fast — this is a genuinely surprising, well-substantiated (exact McNemar p=0.022), and practically consequential finding: it demonstrates concretely that "device testing thus changed the best available deployment," which is precisely the kind of result that justifies the entire premise of the paper. Crucially, the paper does not overclaim this result — it is careful to note the important qualification that within the *original* published roster, the server winner remains unchanged on-device, correctly separating "server rankings transfer" from "the best deployment choice changes once the roster is expanded," which is a subtle and important distinction many weaker papers would blur.

The paper delivers several additional, independently valuable and non-obvious findings rather than resting on a single headline result: that Q4 quantization is a memory optimization but not a reliable speed optimization (with the underlying mechanism plausibly explained); that literal model output behavior (a small model collapsing to answering "A" regardless of precision) can dominate raw accuracy numbers in ways invisible to aggregate benchmarks; that "Discrete-GPU VRAM is therefore a poor proxy for unified-memory demand" on shared-memory edge hardware; and that naive accuracy-per-joule ratios perversely favor a model that has essentially failed the task (SmolLM2-135M) simply because it's cheap to run wrong answers — leading to the well-reasoned methodological prescription that efficiency metrics must be gated by a minimum-capability threshold before being applied. Each of these is a specific, mechanism-level, actionable finding rather than a generic observation, and collectively they build toward the paper's genuinely useful "two-stage deployment procedure" (server shortlist, then mandatory on-device re-testing with a capability gate) — a concrete, transferable methodological contribution to the field.

The inclusion of a cloud-API reference point (Claude Haiku 4.5 scoring 0.756 vs. the best edge configuration's 0.668, with the explicit, amusing, and technically informative detail that Haiku is entirely unscoreable on 6G-Bench because its prose preamble exhausts the 16-token budget before producing valid JSON) is a nice, concrete grounding of the edge results against a practical cloud alternative, giving a reader real operational context ("88% of that accuracy... at no API fee") for the paper's edge-deployment recommendation. This is a well-rounded, practically useful research contribution, falling just short of the very top of this category only because — as the paper's own Limitations section correctly notes — the study is conducted on one board, one llama.cpp version, and a ten-model comparison for the core ranking-transfer tests, which somewhat bounds the generalizability of the specific numeric thresholds even though the methodological lessons are broadly transferable.

## Category 3: Adherence to Track Rules & Disclosure
**Score: 13/15**

The disclosure statement is present and covers the essential elements: it names the primary AI system (Claude Fable 5 via Claude Code) alongside secondary contributors (Claude Opus 5, Claude Sonnet 5, and GPT-5.6 Sol via Codex for clarity-focused review of an earlier draft), and it describes a coherent workflow — Claude Code proposed research directions, the human Research Director selected one, and the AI system then "conducted the experiments, analyzed the results, and drafted and revised the manuscript." The disclosure states plainly that the AI "was informed that its host machine provided access to a Jetson Orin GPU," which is a helpful, concrete detail establishing that the hardware experiments were genuinely run rather than simulated.

The disclosure also describes a real, if lightweight, cross-model review loop: "Codex critiques were returned to Claude Code for self-correction, and Codex later improved the draft's readability." This is a reasonable, disclosed practice, though it is considerably less granular than the most rigorous disclosure statements in this pool — there is no itemized incident log, no SHA-256-hashed provenance ledger for the initial prompt or human interactions, and no explicit accounting of exactly how many rounds of critique occurred or what specific changes resulted, which places this disclosure in the solid-but-not-exceptional tier relative to the strongest examples in this reviewed batch.

The paper's technical core (the ten-page main body) reads with consistent, precise, appropriately technical tone throughout, and there is no obvious seam between sections that would suggest partial human authorship — the in-text acknowledgment of a self-caught v1 scoring bug and its precise, quantified correction is itself a piece of evidence that the disclosed "self-correction" workflow was genuinely operative rather than merely claimed. The named human co-author (a named PhD-track student and a named, real, senior faculty co-author at the University of Toronto) is a notable feature of this paper's authorship model: unlike several other submissions in this pool that explicitly disclaim any human authorship or list only a "Research Director," this paper lists two named humans as full co-authors on the byline, with the disclosure statement then clarifying their actual role was "monitored progress and provided intermediate direction through prompting" — this is a materially different and more conventional authorship posture than the pool's fully AI-attributed submissions, and the panel should weigh this distinction explicitly when calibrating how much credit belongs to the AI system versus the named human authors' oversight and iteration.

On balance, this is an adequate, honest, and specific disclosure that satisfies the track's core requirements, but it does not reach the maximal standard of process-level granularity (hash-verified prompts, itemized revision logs, explicit incident records) demonstrated by the strongest disclosure statements in this pool, which is the basis for a score short of the maximum in this category.

## Category 4: Results, Evaluation, and Honesty on Limitations
**Score: 15/15**

This paper's results reporting is careful, well-calibrated, and consistently honest about the boundaries of what its evidence supports. The paper's pre-registration discipline is a real strength: four concrete expectations (C-E1 through C-E4) are stated before Jetson data collection, and the Results section reports their outcomes with complete transparency, including partial and unsupported findings rather than only confirmations — "C-E1 is only partly supported" (strong accuracy transfer confirmed, but Edge Score transfer at ρ=0.67 falls short of the sub-0.5 prediction), and the expected 1.5–3B accuracy-per-joule optimum from C-E3 is explicitly stated as "not supported" once the correct minimum-accuracy-gated analysis is applied. This willingness to report a pre-registered prediction as unmet, rather than quietly reframing the analysis to manufacture apparent confirmation, is exactly the standard of honesty this rubric rewards most highly.

The paper is unusually careful to distinguish what its data can and cannot establish causally. Beyond the Llama-3.2-1B "reproducible association rather than a proven mechanism" example already noted, the paper explicitly states its own version-1 error and quantifies exactly what changed and what didn't ("no other result changes to four decimal places") — this kind of precise, bounded self-correction, stated with the exact magnitude of the fix, is a much stronger form of honesty than a vague "minor corrections were made" note, and it gives the reader concrete confidence that the correction was properly scoped rather than papered over.

The Limitations section (Section V, under "Limitations") is specific, substantive, and appropriately humbling: it names the single-board, single-runtime-version, two-benchmark scope; flags that "TeleQnA may appear in model training data" (a genuine and important construct-validity concern for any LLM benchmark using a well-known public dataset); notes that energy is measured from internal rails rather than a wall or battery meter and that "each configuration is run once, preventing us from measuring variation across runs, boards, or thermal conditions" — an honest, specific acknowledgment that the paper's point estimates carry unquantified run-to-run and hardware variance; and explicitly notes that "four matched F16/BF16 configurations only partly separate hardware and precision effects," a precise statement of a genuine confound the paper cannot fully disentangle.

The battery-life illustration in Section V (a 10Wh battery supporting "about 1,600 queries if idle power is budgeted separately but only about 950 if the battery powers the whole board... roughly 5W idle load reduces this estimate to about 115 queries" at one query per minute) is a concrete, quantified, and slightly startling demonstration of exactly why the gross/net energy-accounting distinction matters operationally — this is precisely the kind of grounded, consequence-oriented framing that turns an abstract methodological point into a genuinely persuasive, practically relevant conclusion, and it exemplifies the paper's overall commitment to turning every measurement choice into an explicit, examined tradeoff rather than a silently-assumed default.

## Category 5: Structure, Clarity, and Formatting
**Score: 14/15**

The paper follows a clean, logically sequenced structure — Introduction → Prior Evidence and Study Questions (with explicit RQ1–RQ3 and pre-registered C-E1–C-E4 expectations) → Experimental Design (device/model configurations, workloads/scoring, energy/memory/transfer tests) → Results (five clearly labeled findings-forward subsections) → Deployment Procedure and Limitations → Conclusion → Disclosure Statement — and the explicit research-question framing established early is systematically revisited and answered throughout the Results section, giving the paper a traceable, auditable argumentative structure.

The Results subsections are titled with clear, declarative findings ("Server Ranks Shortlist Models but Do Not Preserve the Choice," "The Inference Stack Can Dominate Precision," "Q4 Saves Capacity but Usually Does Not Save Time," "Energy Depends on Workload, Architecture, and Power Mode," "Unified Memory and the Cloud Anchor") that accurately preview their content and make the paper easy to navigate even before parsing the supporting numbers — this is an effective structural device matched by only a few of the strongest papers in this reviewed pool. Table I is dense but well-organized, giving per-model, per-benchmark breakdowns of accuracy, latency, gross/net energy, and memory that directly support essentially every specific numeric claim made in the prose, and Table II's ranking-transfer summary (with explicit correlation coefficients, p-values, and top-agreement flags for seven distinct comparisons) is a valuable, compact companion that lets a reader verify the paper's transfer claims at a glance.

Figures 1 and 2 (accuracy-transfer scatter plot with Wilson intervals, efficiency-rank transfer diagram with explicit rank-change annotations, energy-scaling log-log plot, and the power-mode ablation bar chart) are well-designed and each is read carefully and specifically in the prose rather than merely gestured at — the paper's discussion of Fig. 2(b)'s power-mode results, for instance, walks through the exact net/gross energy values at each of the three power modes and explains the mechanism (shorter execution time repaying higher power draw at MAXN) rather than simply pointing at the chart.

The prose is dense with precise, specific numbers throughout, which is appropriate for a rigorous empirical paper but does impose a real reading-effort cost — a reader needs to track many distinct quantities (accuracy, p50/p95 latency, gross/net energy in joules, memory in GB, across multiple models, benchmarks, precisions, and power modes) simultaneously, and while the paper's subsection structure and tables do meaningfully mitigate this, a slightly more generous use of plain-language recap sentences between dense quantitative passages would have further eased navigation — a minor deduction relative to an otherwise very well-organized paper.

## Category 6: Discussion Potential for the Panel
**Score: 8/10**

This paper offers the panel a genuinely valuable, practically-grounded discussion opportunity centered on a question with real, immediate relevance to the track's own subject matter: as agentic AI systems increasingly get proposed for deployment in resource-constrained network-edge environments, does benchmarking those systems on data-center hardware actually predict their real-world deployment performance? The paper's central finding — that the server-declared best model is *not* the best available edge deployment once the roster is honestly expanded to include a model the source study omitted — is a concrete, well-substantiated, and directly actionable cautionary result that the panel could use to interrogate assumptions embedded in other AI-networking submissions in this pool that evaluate LLM-based agents primarily on server-class or unconstrained compute.

Second, the paper's authorship model — two named human co-authors (a graduate student and a senior, identifiable faculty member) credited on the byline, with a disclosure statement clarifying their actual contribution was prompting, direction, and progress monitoring rather than technical execution — is a genuinely interesting and distinct case for the panel to discuss relative to the pool's more common "AI as sole/primary author, human as Research Director" convention. The panel could productively debate whether crediting real, named, professionally-identifiable humans as co-authors on a paper whose technical content, experiments, and prose were substantially AI-generated represents an appropriate and increasingly common academic norm, or whether it blurs exactly the kind of authorship attribution question this track's disclosure requirements are meant to clarify.

Third, the paper's self-caught scoring bug (the lenient TeleQnA parser crediting echoed option digits) is a small but concrete, specific, and easily explainable example of exactly the kind of implementation-level self-correction this track should want to see documented — precise, quantified, and reported without minimization, giving the panel a clean, low-stakes example to use when discussing what a genuine (versus merely claimed) self-audit looks like in practice.

The paper's discussion value is somewhat narrower than the pool's most structurally dramatic case studies (no formally proven theorem, no self-falsified headline hypothesis, no visible unedited artifact) — its strongest material for live debate is the authorship-model question and the practically consequential but methodologically unsurprising server-vs-edge transfer finding, which, while genuinely useful and well-executed, is less viscerally striking as a discussion centerpiece than the pool's most dramatic positive or negative exemplars.

## Summary Recommendation

This paper should be ranked among the top 11 submissions. It combines genuine, verifiable hardware-level empirical rigor (real power-rail measurements, pinned runtime commits, hash-identified model artifacts) with a precisely targeted and practically important research question — whether data-center-derived LLM rankings actually transfer to constrained edge hardware — and delivers a well-substantiated, appropriately qualified headline finding (a mixture-of-experts model excluded from the source study's ranking outperforms the server-declared winner on-device by a statistically significant margin while using half the energy) alongside several additional, mechanism-level, actionable results about quantization, memory, and power-mode tradeoffs. Its pre-registration discipline, honest reporting of partially- and un-supported hypotheses, precise self-correction of a version-1 scoring bug, and specific, substantive Limitations section together demonstrate a strong standard of empirical honesty. Its chief points of distinction from the very top tier of this pool are a somewhat less granular disclosure statement than the most rigorously provenance-documented submissions, and an authorship model (two named human co-authors credited on the byline) that the panel should discuss explicitly when weighing how much of the paper's quality to attribute to autonomous AI research capability versus human academic oversight — but on technical merit and empirical rigor alone, this paper is a solid, well-earned candidate for the top 11.
