# TPC Review — CNSM 2026 "The Agentic AI Researcher" Track
## Paper: "How Stale Is Too Stale? Residual-Calibrated Service Re-Placement for Edge Services Under Control-Loop Lag"

---

## Category 1: Technical Correctness, Soundness & Red Flags
**Score: 23/25**

This paper's technical formulation is precise, well-grounded in real MEC/O-RAN system characteristics, and mathematically coherent throughout. The control-loop lag decomposition (measurement → decision → actuation) and the correct grounding of ∆ in real architectural tiers (O-RAN near-RT RIC at 10ms–1s, non-RT RIC at seconds-or-more, container orchestrator reconciliation at multi-second intervals) reflects genuine, specific domain knowledge rather than a generic gloss. The information-set formalization (Eq. 2, Fk = σ({Ts}s≤tk−∆) ∪ σ({aj}j<k)) is a correct and careful POMDP-style specification, and the paper's explicit note that including the exact action log "removes the strawman version of the problem" shows real awareness of how to avoid inadvertently stacking the deck in the proposed method's favor.

The paper's central analytical claim — that lag hurts the commit-rule sign flip far more than the assignment ranking, because the ranking depends on the hop-count term (which changes only at ~250m cell-boundary crossings that a sub-2s lag rarely spans) while the commit decision depends on the sign of a small residual margin against a convex, fast-drifting queueing term — is a genuinely sound piece of applied reasoning, and it is not merely asserted: Section VI's F4 result (removing dead-reckoning prediction *improves* accuracy at short-to-medium lag, with mobility extrapolation only marginally useful at 3.2s) is presented as an empirical confirmation of this analytical decomposition, and the paper gives a clean, dimensionally grounded explanation for why ("a 12 m/s vehicle covers 19m in 1.6s against a 250m cell radius"). This kind of hypothesis-then-confirming-ablation structure is a mark of real scientific reasoning rather than post-hoc storytelling.

The SHARP commit rule itself (Eq. 3–4) is mathematically well-constructed: the horizon-scaled uncertainty model σ²_eff(h) = σ₁² + ν²h is a reasonable Brownian-motion-style error-growth assumption, explicitly and honestly flagged in the Limitations as folding "staleness-induced bias into σ1 rather than modelling it" — a specific, technically accurate self-critique of the model's own approximation. The use of MAD (median absolute deviation) rather than RMS for the residual scale estimator is justified with a concrete, quantified example (σ̂1 ≈ 49ms under RMS vs. 0.8ms under MAD at ∆=400ms, causing the RMS variant to "freeze the controller") — this is a specific, checkable engineering detail that reflects genuine understanding of heavy-tailed queueing latency distributions, not a generic textbook citation. The commit rule's degeneracy properties (reducing to a step rule as σ1, ν → 0) are correctly derived and clearly stated.

On the reference list, all seventeen citations are real, well-known, and correctly attributed to this reviewer's knowledge: Polese et al. (O-RAN survey, COMST 2023), Taleb & Ksentini (Follow Me Cloud, IEEE Network 2013), Ouyang et al. (mobility-aware placement, IEEE JSAC 2018), Wang et al. (MDP-based migration, IEEE/ACM ToN 2019), Neely's Lyapunov optimization monograph, Mitzenmacher's two papers on stale-information load balancing and power-of-two-choices (IEEE TPDS 2000/2001), Clark et al. (live VM migration, NSDI 2005), Kalata's α-β tracker (IEEE TAES 1984), and Burns et al. (Borg/Omega/Kubernetes, CACM 2016) are all legitimate, correctly-dated, and topically precise citations. No fabricated venue or implausible metadata was detected. The one genuine red flag in this paper is not the citation list itself but the disclosure statement's own candid admission that "the references were selected from the model's parametric knowledge... the model did not query a live bibliographic database, so volume, issue, and page numbers may contain errors even where the work itself exists" — this is an important, honestly-flagged caveat that the panel should weigh: unlike papers in this pool with disclosed live-verification pipelines, this paper's citations carry a materially higher, self-acknowledged risk of metadata-level (if not existence-level) error.

## Category 2: Novelty, Research Value & Core Strengths
**Score: 19/20**

This paper's central contribution is genuinely novel and sharply targeted: the observation that "almost all published re-placement algorithms are formulated as if ∆ = 0" is a specific, well-substantiated critique of an entire subfield (the Related Work section correctly and specifically shows that Follow-Me-Cloud, MDP formulations, and Lyapunov-drift controllers all assume actuation-time state), and the paper's key analytical insight — that the lag penalty concentrates in the *commit rule* rather than the *assignment optimiser* — is a non-obvious, mechanism-level claim that is neither a trivial restatement of "stale data is bad" nor a generic robustness argument, but a specific structural diagnosis of *where in the pipeline* staleness actually bites. This is exactly the kind of decomposition that turns a vague concern ("what about lag?") into an actionable design principle (calibrate the commit threshold, not the ranking).

The paper's most valuable single empirical finding is F4: that mobility extrapolation — "the standard remedy" for staleness in the mobility-aware placement literature — is actively unhelpful below 3.2s of lag, and the paper gives a crisp, dimensionally-grounded explanation for why (cell-crossing timescales vastly exceed the lag window at realistic vehicular speeds, while location-error noise degrades rather than helps prediction in this regime). This is a genuine "the standard fix doesn't fix the actual problem" finding, explicitly contradicting the emphasis of cited prior work [6], and reporting a result that undercuts an entire remedy the community has converged on is a strong, credible mark of independent empirical reasoning rather than literature-following.

SHARP's tuning-free design is a real, practically valuable engineering contribution: the paper demonstrates (Table IV, F5) that a fixed hysteresis threshold's optimal value swings eightfold (1ms to 8ms) across six realistic operating points that an operator cannot know in advance, while SHARP tracks this swing automatically via online residual calibration, achieving 0.35pp mean regret against a per-point oracle-tuned threshold — this is a genuinely useful robustness-without-tuning result with direct operational value, and the explicit framing (the case for SHARP is "robustness, not peak performance") is an appropriately modest and accurate characterization of what the method actually delivers, rather than an inflated claim of dominance.

F1's quantified operational guideline — "halving ∆ buys 1.4–2.6× more SLO than substituting the best available algorithm for the worst load-aware one at fixed ∆" — is a genuinely actionable, quantified piece of engineering guidance with a clear practical recommendation (prioritize placing the xApp on the near-RT RIC over algorithmic sophistication), which is exactly the kind of specific, decision-relevant conclusion that elevates a paper's research value beyond "we tried something and it sort of worked." The paper's novelty ceiling is that the underlying components (α-β tracking, MAD-based robust scale estimation, hysteresis-based commit rules, discrete-event simulation) are each individually standard techniques; the contribution is their careful combination and the specific mechanism-level lag-decomposition insight, not a new algorithmic primitive — but this is legitimate, well-executed applied research value rather than a limitation that undercuts the paper's core claims.

## Category 3: Adherence to Track Rules & Disclosure
**Score: 12/15**

The disclosure statement is present, structured, and unusually candid about its own gaps — but it contains a striking and consequential feature not seen in any other paper in this pool: multiple explicit `[PLACEHOLDER]` fields left unfilled in the submitted document itself, covering "degree and nature of human oversight of the session," "institutional affiliation under which this submission is made," whether the execution container was contended, the "simulator source and raw logs" repository URL, responsibility for correcting bibliographic metadata errors, and — most notably — an entire unfilled "Funding, competing interests, prior/concurrent submission, and ethics review" section. This is a substantive process gap: several of these fields (institutional affiliation, repository URL, conflict-of-interest/ethics disclosures) are not optional niceties but are typically required for a genuine conference submission, and their absence suggests either that this document was submitted in an intermediate, unfinished state, or that the disclosure convention deliberately leaves them for a human to complete — the paper itself does not clarify which.

Despite these gaps, what *is* disclosed is genuinely thorough and valuable. The "Conflict of interest in the evaluation" subsection is an unusually direct and self-aware piece of methodological honesty: it explicitly names the structural problem ("the same agent designed both the proposed method and the evaluation that judges it") and describes three concrete, auditable mitigations (baseline hyperparameters tuned on seeds disjoint from evaluation seeds; inclusion of an oracle-tuned FH-opt variant that SHARP does *not* beat; explicit commitment to report baseline-wins, ablation failures, and no-separation regimes with equal prominence in the main results rather than deferring them). This is a genuinely sophisticated act of self-auditing that goes beyond what most of the disclosure statements in this pool attempt, and the paper's own results (F3, F6) substantiate that these commitments were actually honored.

The "Bibliographic verification" subsection is a valuable and rare piece of honesty: rather than claiming citations were checked against a live database (as several other papers in this pool do), this paper states plainly that citations were drawn from parametric knowledge without live verification, and that "reviewers should treat the reference list as unverified metadata attached to correctly identified works." This is a materially more cautious and arguably more honest disclosure stance than papers that claim verification pipelines the panel cannot independently audit — but it does mean this paper's citation risk profile is real and self-acknowledged rather than mitigated.

The technical core itself reads with complete AI-tone consistency and no evidence of human editing, matching the disclosure's claim that "no human wrote, edited, or reviewed any sentence of this paper." On balance, this disclosure is honest and substantively informative where it speaks, but the volume and nature of unfilled placeholder fields — particularly the entirely empty ethics/funding/competing-interests section — is a real adherence gap relative to what a complete camera-ready disclosure should contain, and this is weighed as a moderate deduction rather than a minor one.

## Category 4: Results, Evaluation, and Honesty on Limitations
**Score: 15/15**

This paper's results reporting is exceptional and, alongside the strongest papers in this pool, represents a benchmark for honest empirical writing. The "Execution statement" at the top of Section V — "Every number reported in this paper was produced by executing the simulator described here; none is analytical, extrapolated, or taken from another source" — is a strong, falsifiable claim about result provenance, and the paper's own Conclusion follows through on this standard by reporting its losses with the same prominence as its wins: "It does not beat a per-operating-point-tuned threshold — it loses by 0.42 pp at the nominal point." This loss is not hedged, minimized, or buried; it is stated plainly in the Abstract's final sentences and again in the Conclusion's second sentence, exactly where a reader would expect to find the paper's strongest claim.

The paper's F3 finding (a tuned fixed threshold beats SHARP at every ∆ from 200–1600ms, with the differences statistically significant via paired t-tests: t=−13.4 at 400ms) is reported with complete numerical transparency and an honest mechanistic explanation ("SHARP is, at these settings, slightly too conservative... a wrong move is cheap (150ms) and gets corrected two epochs later"), rather than being explained away. F6 similarly reports a genuine null finding — at high utilization (ρ̄=0.81), "all four policies fall within 0.8pp of one another... even the zero-lag ORACLE only reaches 18.1%" — and draws the correct, generalizable methodological lesson from it: "Papers reporting placement gains should state their ρ̄, because the same algorithm can look transformative at ρ̄=0.52... and irrelevant at ρ̄=0.81." This is a genuinely valuable piece of meta-scientific guidance extracted from the paper's own null result, exactly the kind of insight that distinguishes rigorous empirical work from result-mining.

The Limitations section (VII) is exceptionally thorough, itemized into seven specific, individually substantive threats rather than generic hedging: it flags that the M/G/1-PS queueing formula's smoothness biases σ̂1 downward, making SHARP "more aggressive than it should be" and predicting the direction the F3 loss to FH would move under a higher-fidelity simulator; it identifies that a single global σ (rather than per-site calibration) is "the most probable cause of the 0.42pp deficit at OP1," directly and specifically diagnosing the mechanism behind the paper's own worst result; it flags that the error-growth model folds staleness-bias into σ1 rather than modeling it explicitly; and it explicitly states the paper did not compare against MPC or deep-RL controllers, bounding the claim accordingly ("a calibrated commit rule removes the tuning burden at ≈0.35pp cost, not that it is the best achievable policy"). This is diagnostic, mechanism-level limitation-reporting of a caliber matched by very few other papers in this entire pool.

The disclosed conflict-of-interest mitigations (Section III of the Appendix, cross-referenced above) are directly substantiated by the paper's actual results — the oracle-tuned FH-opt baseline genuinely is included and genuinely does beat SHARP in the reported comparison, and the "cases where we lose" (F3, F4's dead-reckoning ablation, F6) are genuinely given equal structural prominence in Section VI rather than relegated to a brief aside. This alignment between the disclosure's stated methodological commitments and the paper's actual reported content is strong, checkable evidence of honest execution rather than a merely asserted standard.

## Category 5: Structure, Clarity, and Formatting
**Score: 14/15**

The paper follows a rigorous, well-sequenced structure — Introduction (with a five-item contribution list, C1–C5, that accurately previews the entire paper) → Related Work → System Model and Problem Formulation → Proposed Approach (SHARP) → Evaluation (setup, baselines, metrics) → Results and Analysis (six labeled findings, F1–F6) → Limitations (seven itemized threats) → Conclusion → Appendix (Disclosure) — and the parallel C1–C5/F1–F6 labeling scheme is an effective structural device that lets a reader directly trace each contribution claim to the specific finding that substantiates or complicates it, which is a more disciplined cross-referencing practice than most papers in this pool employ.

The mathematical formulation is dense but precisely notated and consistently used throughout — Fk, σ_eff(h), Hu, gˆu, θ recur with stable meaning from Section III through the Results — and Algorithm 1's pseudocode is detailed and specific enough (including a genuinely useful implementation note about incremental load updates preventing herding, directly tied back to the Mitzenmacher citation) to be reproducible in principle. Table I's parameter listing is comprehensive and specific (down to accurate O-RAN-consistent values for ℓacc, ℓhop, and realistic SLO deadlines), and Tables II–IV are well-designed, each directly supporting a specific labeled finding in the prose without redundant restatement.

The prose is dense and technically demanding — this is unmistakably a paper written for an expert MEC/networking audience, with minimal hand-holding — which is appropriate for the venue but does impose real cognitive load: parsing the exact relationship between Eq. 1's latency model, Eq. 3's error-growth model, and Eq. 4's commit rule in one continuous read requires careful attention, and a reader unfamiliar with M/G/1 processor-sharing queueing or α-β tracking would need to consult the cited background material to fully follow the derivation. This is a reasonable cost of genuine technical depth within a tight page budget, and the six labeled findings (F1–F6) in Section VI do an excellent job of periodically re-grounding the reader in plain-language takeaways after each technical result, which meaningfully offsets the density. The one clarity/formatting concern, addressed more fully in Category 3, is the unresolved placeholder text visible in the final Appendix — a formatting completeness issue in the disclosure section specifically, not in the technical core, which is otherwise clean throughout.

## Category 6: Discussion Potential for the Panel
**Score: 9/10**

This paper offers the panel an unusually rich and multi-layered discussion opportunity. Most directly, the visible `[PLACEHOLDER]` fields — left unfilled in the submitted disclosure, including an entirely empty funding/competing-interests/ethics-review section — are a concrete, debatable artifact that the panel should discuss explicitly: does a track evaluating "the Agentic AI Researcher" want to reward a paper that transparently flags exactly which institutional/administrative fields it cannot itself complete (a genuinely honest acknowledgment of the limits of AI-only authorship, since an AI agent genuinely cannot supply an institutional affiliation, funding source, or ethics-review status that doesn't exist), or does this expose a process gap the track should require to be resolved before submission? This is a distinct and genuinely novel governance question this specific paper raises more starkly than any other in the pool.

Second, the paper's central technical finding — that the standard remedy for staleness in the mobility-placement literature (motion extrapolation) does not actually address the dominant source of staleness-induced error (load drift, not position drift) — is a substantive, well-reasoned challenge to an established subfield's default assumption, and the panel could use this as a case study in whether an autonomous agent, working from parametric knowledge and a self-built simulator rather than live literature verification, can still produce a genuinely credible and non-trivial critique of prior work. This connects directly to the disclosed uncertainty about bibliographic verification: the panel might discuss whether a citation list built from parametric knowledge (candidly disclosed as such) is more or less trustworthy than one claimed to be live-verified but not independently auditable by the panel.

Third, the explicit, itemized "Conflict of interest in the evaluation" disclosure — an AI system naming its own structural conflict of interest (designing both method and evaluation) and describing specific, auditable mitigations — is a valuable and directly comparable artifact against similar self-audit practices seen elsewhere in this pool (e.g., the discarded self-authored keyword baseline in the log-diagnosis paper), and the panel could use this paper as one data point in a broader discussion of what a credible self-audit protocol should minimally contain when an AI system cannot rely on an independent human or second-agent check.

The paper's discussion value is somewhat tempered by the fact that its core empirical content, while rigorous, does not contain as singularly dramatic a "smoking gun" as some other papers in this pool (no formally proven theorem, no self-falsified pre-registered hypothesis, no visible leftover template artifact in the technical core) — its most debate-worthy feature is specifically the disclosure completeness question, which is a real and valuable discussion point but a narrower one than the technical-content-driven discussions the pool's top papers offer.

## Summary Recommendation

This paper should be ranked among the top 11 submissions. It identifies a genuinely sharp and underexplored problem — that published MEC re-placement algorithms almost universally ignore control-loop lag — and answers it with a well-reasoned mechanism-level diagnosis (the lag penalty concentrates in the commit rule, not the ranking), a carefully engineered tuning-free solution (SHARP), and an exceptionally honest empirical evaluation that reports its losses (a tuned fixed threshold beats it at several operating points) with the same prominence as its wins, complete with a diagnostic, mechanism-level Limitations section that explains *why* each shortfall occurs rather than merely disclosing that it does. Its chief weakness relative to the strongest papers in this pool is a disclosure statement containing multiple unfilled placeholder fields — including an entirely empty funding/competing-interests/ethics section — which is a real process-completeness gap even though what is disclosed (particularly the explicit conflict-of-interest mitigations) is unusually thorough and substantively honest. On the strength of its technical rigor, genuine novelty, and exemplary results-honesty, this paper merits a place in the top 11, while the panel should specifically flag its disclosure completeness as a discussion point for what the track should require going forward.
