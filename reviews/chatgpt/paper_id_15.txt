## Category 1: Technical Correctness, Soundness & Red Flags

**Score: 17/25**

The central technical formulation is meaningful and mostly sound. Modeling the controller as restricted to the lagged information set \(\mathcal{F}_k=\sigma(\{T_s\}_{s\le t_k-\Delta})\cup\sigma(\{a_j\}_{j<k})\) is a clean way to separate telemetry staleness from uncertainty about the controller's own actions, and explicitly keeping the action log exact avoids an artificial “forgotten in-flight migration” problem.  The M/G/1-PS-inspired latency approximation, migration downtime, transfer-slot contention, and direct SLO-violation objective also form a coherent simplified MEC model. However, SHARP's calibration equations contain a concrete mathematical inconsistency: \(\sigma_1\) is described as the residual scale measured at horizon \(h_1\), and \(\nu^2=(\sigma_L^2-\sigma_1^2)/(h_2-h_1)\), but Eq. (3) then defines \(\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^2(h)=\sigma_1^2+\nu^2h\). If \(\sigma_1\) is the value at \(h_1\), the consistent interpolation is \(\sigma_1^2+\nu^2(h-h_1)\), or equivalently an appropriately shifted intercept.  The error may be numerically modest for \(h_1=0.2\) s, but it directly affects the paper's defining uncertainty model.

There are several additional technical overstatements. The claimed “MAD” estimator is written as \(1.4826\,\mathrm{median}|r|\), whereas the conventional median absolute deviation is based on \(|r-\mathrm{median}(r)|\); using the former implicitly assumes zero-centered residuals even though the Limitations section later acknowledges staleness-induced bias.  Equation (4) also does not literally integrate deadline-crossing probability across the residence horizon as claimed: it evaluates one probability difference using \(\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}(H_u)\) and multiplies it by \(H_u\). Furthermore, saying SHARP “has no tuned constant” is too broad: the migration threshold is adaptive, but the method still fixes \(\alpha,\beta\), EWMA behavior, \(h_2=4\) s, residence clipping, candidate-set size, and an apparently undefined \(\epsilon\) in Algorithm 1.  There is also a numerical wording error in the headline result: at \(\Delta=6.4\) s SHARP is actually the best of the three load-aware policies at 10.23%, not FH at 10.71%; therefore the “best policy rises 11.8× from 0.91% to 10.71%” statement is not strictly correct. 

The bibliography itself does **not show obvious fabricated papers or fake DOIs**. Core references such as the O-RAN survey, Wang et al.'s MDP service-migration paper, Mitzenmacher's stale-information analysis, Machen et al.'s live MEC migration work, and Kalata's \(\alpha\)-\(\beta\) tracking paper are genuine and the quoted journal metadata are broadly correct.  ([ResearchGate][1]) The red flag is that the paper explicitly admits the bibliography was generated from parametric knowledge without live verification, and reference [2] does not even provide a version or year for ETSI GS MEC 013.  More importantly, the literature review misses directly relevant 2026 uncertainty-aware fog/MEC migration work published before submission, including a Digital-Twin-based framework that explicitly gates migration using prediction uncertainty. ([TechScience][2]) Thus the references are mostly real, but the novelty claim is not supported by a sufficiently current or exhaustive literature search.

## Category 2: Novelty, Research Value & Core Strengths

**Score: 14/20**

The paper's strongest idea is the decision to treat **staleness primarily as a commit-confidence problem rather than merely a state-prediction problem**. This is a useful perspective for autonomous orchestration. Instead of replacing the whole placement optimizer with a more complicated predictor or RL policy, SHARP asks whether the predicted improvement is sufficiently robust to justify a costly migration, and adapts that decision using prediction residuals measured by the controller itself.  That is operationally attractive: the mechanism is small, explainable, can sit around an existing candidate-site selector, and naturally reduces churn as uncertainty increases.

The novelty is nevertheless more moderate than the manuscript implies. Adaptive uncertainty-aware migration, confidence-gated orchestration, mobility/load prediction, and robust edge decision-making already have active literature; for example, a May 2026 fog-computing paper combines mobility/load forecasting with an uncertainty-sensitive mechanism to regulate proactive migration. ([TechScience][2]) SHARP's distinctive contribution is therefore narrower: calibrating the *commit hysteresis* from the controller's own latency residuals and explicitly studying the effect of measure-to-actuate lag. That is a plausible contribution, but the statement that published re-placement work is “almost” universally \(\Delta=0\) is insufficiently substantiated by the relatively short, partly dated related-work section. 

The most valuable research result may actually be the **design-space characterization**, not SHARP's nominal performance. The paper shows that lag can dominate the choice among several simple load-aware policies, that aggressive reoptimization creates costly churn, that mobility extrapolation can be counterproductive at realistic sub-second/mid-second horizons, and that no migration policy can compensate for a genuinely capacity-limited regime.  These are useful hypotheses for network/service-management practice. If validated on a real MEC/RIC testbed with empirically calibrated telemetry delays, migration times, and workloads, the “control-loop speed versus algorithm sophistication” trade-off could be more impactful than the specific SHARP formula.

## Category 3: Adherence to Track Rules & Disclosure

**Score: 3/15**

Topically, the submission is an excellent fit: MEC service placement, orchestration, stale telemetry, and 5G/6G edge services fall squarely within the allowed CNSM experimental-track scope. The disclosure also identifies Claude Opus 5 as the autonomous researcher and explicitly states that problem selection, modeling, implementation, experiments, analysis, and prose were model-generated with no human-written or edited technical material.  Claude Opus 5 is a genuine Anthropic model released in July 2026. ([Anthropic][3])

Unfortunately, the submission violates one of the clearest formal rules: the uploaded PDF is **six pages**, whereas the CFP imposes a hard maximum of five pages including both references and the mandatory Disclosure Statement. ([CNSM Conference][4]) Page 6 contains a substantial portion of the disclosure rather than an accidental blank page, so this cannot reasonably be treated as formatting noise. The paper therefore fails an objective submission requirement irrespective of its technical merits.

The Disclosure Statement is also materially incomplete. CNSM requires the LLM(s), **exact initial prompting strategy**, code/experiment framework, and constraints. ([CNSM Conference][4]) Here there is no initial prompt at all, and multiple fields literally remain `[PLACEHOLDER]`: degree/nature of human oversight, institutional affiliation, execution contention information, repository URL, responsibility for bibliography correction, and funding/competing-interest/prior-submission/ethics information.  The disclosure is commendably candid about these missing elements, but candor does not satisfy the mandatory appendix. Most seriously for this track, the unresolved human-oversight placeholder prevents independent assessment of whether the zero-human-in-the-loop rule was actually followed.

## Category 4: Results, Evaluation, and Honesty on Limitations

**Score: 11/15**

The simulation campaign is substantially better designed than many synthetic studies. Ten evaluation seeds are paired across every policy and lag value, three disjoint seeds are reserved for baseline tuning, migration churn consumes explicit per-site transfer capacity, and the paper compares STATIC, FOLLOW, reactive greedy, fixed hysteresis, per-operating-point tuned hysteresis, SHARP, and a zero-lag reference.  The operating-point study additionally varies lag, load, and migration duration/state size rather than demonstrating the proposal at a single hand-selected setting. The result that SHARP often loses to the fixed threshold at the nominal/mid-lag points but substantially reduces migration rate is believable and scientifically more useful than an implausible universal win.

The statistical treatment is adequate but not particularly strong. Ten seeds produce relatively narrow-looking confidence intervals, yet the paper conducts several paired t-tests across lag values without reporting a multiplicity correction; the borderline reversal at 6.4 s (\(t=2.48\)) should therefore not be emphasized as strongly as the very large mid-lag effects.  FH-OPT is also described as an “oracle” threshold even though it is tuned on only three held-out seeds and can perform worse than FH-FIX on evaluation data, as happens at the high-load operating point.  This is an optimized held-out baseline, not an oracle in the conventional sense. Likewise, the strong operational recommendation that an operator should choose a near-RT xApp over a “smarter” non-RT rApp is too general for evidence from one synthetic MEC-placement simulator; those control domains are not interchangeable merely by latency.

The honesty about limitations is otherwise a major strength. The paper explicitly identifies the simplified M/G/1-PS model, global rather than site-specific calibration, Brownian zero-mean error assumption, missing bandwidth contention and pre-copy behavior, unrealistic grid/mobility model, lack of MPC/RL baselines, exact-action-log assumption, and absence of any testbed or real RIC.  It even diagnoses plausible reasons why its own method loses and says absolute violation rates are not predictive. The disclosure further acknowledges the structural conflict created when the same AI designs both the method and evaluation and describes mitigations that deliberately retain negative results.  This substantially raises confidence in the presentation, even though it cannot compensate for the mathematical calibration issue and synthetic external validity.

## Category 5: Structure, Clarity, and Formatting

**Score: 10/15**

The technical manuscript itself is very well organized. It follows a clear chain from motivation and related work through the lagged-information model, SHARP, experimental methodology, six findings, detailed limitations, and conclusion. The Abstract is unusually informative and reports an unfavorable nominal comparison up front rather than hiding it.  The C1–C5 contribution structure and F1–F6 results structure make a dense paper relatively easy to navigate.

Explanations are generally excellent. The paper clearly distinguishes assignment ranking from migration commitment, explains why migration downtime enters the SLO objective directly, and makes the role of the exact action log explicit. Algorithm 1 gives enough detail to understand the implementation, while Tables II–IV make the lag sweep, ablation, and robustness trade-offs inspectable.   Particularly good is the repeated distinction between peak performance and tuning-free robustness: the manuscript does not rhetorically turn a 0.42 pp loss into a victory.

The formatting/completion problems are nevertheless severe. The six-page length violates the track format, and the final page visibly contains unresolved submission placeholders.  This creates the unusual situation where the *technical* writing deserves a very high clarity score but the submitted artifact is plainly unfinished. There are also a few presentational overclaims—“no tuned constant,” “best policy rises 11.8×,” and the near-RT-versus-non-RT recommendation—that a final editorial pass should have caught. In a normal conference review these would be minor-to-moderate issues; in an experimental track specifically testing autonomous end-to-end artifact production, the inability to finish the mandatory metadata and fit the page budget is itself an important quality signal.

## Category 6: Discussion Potential for the Panel

**Score: 10/10**

This paper is an excellent panel artifact because it simultaneously demonstrates impressive scientific behavior and conspicuous autonomous-workflow failure. The AI independently formulated a genuinely relevant MEC control problem, built and executed a 500+-run simulation campaign, tuned baselines on disjoint seeds, reported cases where the proposed method loses, performed ablations that invalidate one of its own intuitions, and wrote a sophisticated limitations section.  That is a strong example of an autonomous model doing more than composing plausible prose.

At the same time, expert review reveals exactly the kinds of errors that polished AI-generated science can conceal: a shifted-origin inconsistency in the residual-variance equation, a nonstandard “MAD” definition, overstatement of “no tuning,” a small but concrete headline arithmetic/selection error, and an incomplete survey that misses contemporary uncertainty-aware edge migration. The paper is therefore useful for discussing whether an autonomous researcher can maintain **global mathematical consistency** across a complex artifact rather than merely generating individually plausible equations and experiments.

The most striking panel issue is procedural. An AI capable of generating hundreds of simulations, detailed negative-result analysis, and an unusually thoughtful conflict-of-interest statement nevertheless submits a six-page paper to a five-page track and leaves literal `[PLACEHOLDER]` fields in the mandatory disclosure.  That contrast is highly instructive: the model demonstrates substantial scientific reasoning while failing simple completion and compliance checks that could have been mechanically verified. For the purpose of the Agentic AI Researcher experiment, this is almost an ideal illustration of why autonomous research requires not only reasoning agents but also reliable artifact-validation and submission-governance layers.

# FINAL SCORE: **65/100**

**Top-11 recommendation:** **No, despite strong technical promise.** On scientific content alone, this could plausibly compete for the top 11: the stale-control-loop question is relevant, SHARP is a credible and interpretable idea, the simulator is comparatively disciplined, and the paper reports negative findings unusually well. As the **submitted experimental artifact**, however, I would rank it outside the top 11 because it violates the hard five-page limit, omits the mandatory exact prompting disclosure, contains multiple unresolved placeholders, provides no usable repository link, and leaves human oversight unspecified. In addition, the core uncertainty equation has a real calibration inconsistency and the headline “best policy” factor is numerically misstated. Those issues are especially important in a track whose purpose is to evaluate whether an AI can autonomously complete the entire research-and-submission workflow.
