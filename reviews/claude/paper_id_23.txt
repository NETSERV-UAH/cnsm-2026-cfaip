# TPC Review — CNSM 2026 "The Agentic AI Researcher" Track
## Paper: "Neuro-Symbolic Orchestration for Safe Autonomous NetOps Using LLMs and Fuzzy Control"

---

## Category 1: Technical Correctness, Soundness & Red Flags
**Score: 14/25**

The paper's fuzzy-logic formulation is textbook-correct as far as the mathematics goes: the Mamdani inference structure (fuzzification via trapezoidal/triangular membership functions, Eq. 4–5; rule activation via minimum t-norm, Eq. 6; aggregation via maximum s-norm, Eq. 7; centroid defuzzification, Eq. 8) is a standard, correctly-notated formulation that accurately reflects Mamdani's original 1975 approach, and the paper correctly cites the foundational Mamdani & Assilian paper. The three-tier orchestration policy (Approve/Supervise/Block mapped to defuzzified risk thresholds) is a reasonable, interpretable decision architecture, and the core conceptual idea — using a deterministic fuzzy layer as a non-bypassable gate on LLM-proposed actions, informed jointly by physical/logical telemetry and the LLM's own self-reported action-impact score — is a coherent, sensible safety-architecture pattern broadly consistent with the guardrail/shielding literature it cites (GuardAgent, VeriGuard).

However, the central technical vulnerability of this entire architecture is one the paper never interrogates: the safety-critical "Action Impact" score I is *self-assessed by the same LLM that proposes the mitigation* (Eq. 2, I = φLLM(x)), extracted via JSON schema enforcement rather than independently verified against the actual content or consequence of the proposed action. This is a significant, unaddressed soundness gap — a hallucinating or miscalibrated LLM has no external mechanism preventing it from also mis-reporting its own action's invasiveness (e.g., labeling a disruptive interface shutdown as I=0.2), and because the fuzzy rules gate heavily on I (e.g., "IF CRC is Severo AND I is Leve THEN R is Bloquear" — critically, this exact rule *requires* the LLM to have under-reported the impact of a genuinely severe action for the safety mechanism to correctly fire), the entire safety guarantee is only as trustworthy as the same untrusted model's self-report. A rigorous treatment of this architecture would need to independently validate I against the *content* of suggested_mitigation (e.g., via a separate classifier or rule-based parser detecting "shutdown," "reset," etc.), and the absence of any such cross-check, or even acknowledgment of this circularity, is the paper's most serious technical soundness gap.

The experimental design itself raises a second, related soundness concern: the dataset is described as "synthesized" with specific hand-authored ranges for each fault layer (e.g., L1: 60≤CRC≤450 with 5%≤RX≤30%), but the paper gives no detail on how the LLM's action_impact scores were actually generated for these 600 synthetic events — whether via 600 real LLaMA 3.2:1b API calls, or via some scripted/simulated approximation of what such calls would produce. Given the fine-grained, precisely-tabulated results (e.g., the L1 scenario's exact 48.6%/51.4% split, traced to "the fuzzy controller activated the safety rule" for specific instances), the paper's methodology section does not adequately establish whether these are genuine LLM outputs at all, which is a material gap for a paper whose central technical claim rests on LLM-fuzzy interaction.

On the reference list, the citations are plausible and mostly verifiable: Mamdani & Assilian (1975, foundational and correctly cited), Clemm et al.'s telemetry survey (IEEE COMST 2020), and the GuardAgent/VeriGuard arXiv papers (2406.09187, 2510.05156) are real and topically precise. However, reference [4] (Djurayev, Matkurbonov, Khojiakbar, "Analysis of a Model for Improving the Efficiency of Routing Control...Based on Fuzzy Logic," *Communications*, 2023) uses an unusual DOI pattern (10.11648/j.com...) from a lesser-known open-access publisher that this reviewer cannot independently corroborate with confidence, and reference [5] (Qayyum et al., "LaMA-SON," IEEE Network 2025) and [6] (ComAgent, arXiv 2601.19607, 2026) are recent enough that their existence and precise framing cannot be fully verified by this reviewer either — none shows an outright implausible pattern, but the panel should treat these as meriting a spot-check given the absence of any disclosed citation-verification mechanism in this paper's disclosure statement (discussed further in Category 3).

## Category 2: Novelty, Research Value & Core Strengths
**Score: 10/20**

The paper's core architectural idea — pairing an LLM's semantic/diagnostic reasoning with a deterministic, interpretable fuzzy-logic gate that governs whether the LLM's proposed action is actually allowed to execute — is a reasonable and legitimate contribution to the LLM-agent-safety literature, and the paper correctly and specifically situates itself relative to adjacent work: it distinguishes its approach from domain-independent LLM-agent guardrails (GuardAgent, VeriGuard) by explicitly incorporating networking-specific physical and logical telemetry into the safety decision, and from prior agentic-networking work (LaMA-SON, ComAgent) by noting those focus on "task completion and optimization rather than enforcing a deterministic safety boundary." This differentiation is specific and reasonably well-argued rather than a vague novelty claim.

The three-tier Approve/Supervise/Block orchestration policy, and the explicit design choice to route ambiguous cases to Human-in-the-Loop supervision rather than force a binary allow/deny decision, is a sensible and practically-minded safety pattern, and the paper's headline comparison — that classic fuzzy logic without LLM impact-awareness is "over-permissive" (94.5% approval, 0% blocking) while the neuro-symbolic combination achieves a more calibrated 41.7%/55.3%/3.0% split — is, in principle, exactly the kind of finding that would demonstrate the value of combining semantic reasoning with deterministic control.

However, as established in Category 1, this headline finding's credibility is undermined by the unaddressed circularity of the self-assessed impact score: the paper's entire narrative of "successfully containing tactical hallucinations" (Section V-C-1) depends on the LLM under-reporting the invasiveness of a genuinely disruptive action so that the specific gating rule can trigger — but the paper never establishes or tests *why* the LLM would reliably do this (report I=Leve for actions the fuzzy system should block) rather than the alternative failure mode (the LLM over-reporting its own impact, or under-reporting it in a way the fuzzy rules don't happen to catch). Without any adversarial robustness testing of the LLM's self-assessment against genuinely diverse or deceptive outputs, the paper's demonstrated "success" may simply reflect a favorably-calibrated synthetic dataset rather than a genuinely robust safety architecture, which substantially limits the paper's actual research value as a safety contribution.

The paper also does not meaningfully engage with an obvious alternative and arguably more robust design: rather than trusting the LLM's self-reported impact score, a genuinely more rigorous neuro-symbolic architecture might independently classify or verify the *content* of the proposed mitigation against a rule-based action taxonomy (e.g., detecting keywords like "shutdown," "reset," "no ip route" in suggested_mitigation) rather than relying on the same model's own numeric self-assessment. This is not merely a hypothetical improvement — it is the natural, more defensible version of the paper's own stated goal ("separating semantic reasoning from the authorization of potentially disruptive actions"), and its absence, without discussion, limits how much the paper actually advances the state of the art in LLM-agent safety for NetOps beyond a reasonable but under-scrutinized architectural sketch.

## Category 3: Adherence to Track Rules & Disclosure
**Score: 8/15**

A disclosure statement is present and names several tools used: Google Gemini as "the primary LLM used for comprehensive manuscript drafting, LaTeX scaffolding, and controller script development," OpenAI ChatGPT for "bibliographic review and comparative methodology refinement," Consensus.app for literature discovery, and the locally-deployed LLaMA 3.2:1b as the in-system diagnostic inference engine. This satisfies the basic requirement of naming the models used.

However, this disclosure is materially less specific and less verifiable than the strongest disclosure statements in this reviewed pool, in several concrete ways. First, it does not specify what "reviewed the generated content for scientific validity, ensuring factual rigor" actually entailed in practice — there is no itemized account of what, if anything, the human Research Directors changed, checked, or caught, unlike papers in this pool that document specific corrections, discarded pilots, or adversarial-audit findings. Second, and more importantly, the disclosure does not specify any citation-verification mechanism (no mention of checking references against Crossref, DOI resolution, or any other bibliographic ground-truth source), which is directly relevant to the Category 1 concern about unverifiable references — several other strong papers in this pool explicitly disclose and describe such verification steps, and this paper's silence on the point is a meaningful gap given that "bibliographic review" was assigned to a general-purpose chatbot (ChatGPT) rather than to a tool-augmented, source-checking pipeline.

Third, the disclosure does not clarify how the 600-event experimental dataset's LLM impact-assessment scores were actually generated — whether the local LLaMA 3.2:1b model was genuinely queried 600 times to produce real action_impact scores for the synthetic telemetry vectors, or whether these values were otherwise scripted or approximated. Given that this is the single most safety-critical and empirically central quantity in the entire paper, the disclosure's silence on exactly how it was produced is a real transparency gap relative to the standard set by papers in this pool that provide detailed execution accounting (call counts, token counts, wall-clock times) for every experimental run.

The technical core reads with reasonably consistent tone throughout, and there is no obvious seam suggesting partial human authorship of specific passages — the prose register (a fairly standard academic IEEE conference style) is stable from Introduction through Conclusion. The paper does at least specify the prompting strategy for the diagnostic agent (structured zero-shot with strict JSON schema enforcement) with reasonable precision. On balance, this disclosure meets the minimum bar for the track's requirement but falls notably short of the granularity, specificity, and self-audit practices demonstrated by the strongest disclosure statements in this reviewed pool, warranting a score in the lower-middle portion of this category's range.

## Category 4: Results, Evaluation, and Honesty on Limitations
**Score: 5/15**

The paper's results are presented with consistently uniform, unqualified confidence, and this is a significant weakness relative to the standard this rubric rewards. Every comparison in the paper favors the proposed Neuro-Symbolic architecture: it "outperforms" static thresholds, classic ML, and classic fuzzy logic across every reported metric and every scenario, and the paper's own framing throughout uses triumphant, unhedged language ("clear superiority," "eliminates the catastrophic risks," "completely preventing inappropriate software modifications," "selective autonomy"). There is no instance anywhere in the paper of a result that complicates, partially contradicts, or fails to support the proposed method — a pattern that, across this entire reviewed pool, has consistently correlated with the "magical outcomes" failure mode this rubric explicitly warns against.

Critically, there is **no Limitations section anywhere in this paper.** Every other paper in this reviewed pool that scores well on this criterion devotes a dedicated, substantive section to acknowledging what its evaluation does not establish — construct validity concerns, external validity bounds, statistical power issues, or specific failure modes of its own proposed method. This paper's Conclusion moves directly from a bulleted list of triumphant findings to the paper's end, with no acknowledgment that: the dataset is entirely synthetic and hand-parameterized rather than drawn from or validated against real network incidents; the safety-critical self-assessed impact score I is never independently validated or adversarially stress-tested (as discussed in Categories 1–2); the specific fuzzy rule thresholds and membership-function boundaries appear to be manually tuned by the same process that generated the evaluation, raising an unaddressed risk of the rules being fit to produce favorable results on this exact synthetic dataset; or that a single small quantized model (LLaMA 3.2:1b) was tested, with no discussion of whether these findings would generalize to other model families or sizes.

The absence of any confidence intervals, repeated trials, or variance reporting across the 600-event evaluation is also notable: every percentage in Tables I–II is reported as a single point estimate with no indication of how sensitive these figures might be to the particular random seed or synthetic-data generation used, and — as with the concern raised in Category 1 — no discussion of whether re-running the same 600-event generation process with a different seed would produce meaningfully different numbers. Given that the entire evaluation dataset was synthesized by the same research pipeline being evaluated, this absence of any robustness or sensitivity check is a meaningful evaluative gap.

The paper's own narrative descriptions of "success" cases (e.g., Section V-C-1's account of the L1 scenario) do at least specify the exact mechanism by which the safety rule fires, which gives the reader some traceability into how the reported numbers were derived from the stated rule base — this is a modest positive relative to a pure black-box results table — but it does not compensate for the complete absence of any critical self-examination, negative finding, or acknowledged limitation anywhere in the paper's substantive text, which is the primary driver of this category's low score.

## Category 5: Structure, Clarity, and Formatting
**Score: 11/15**

The paper follows a standard and complete IEEE conference structure — Abstract → Introduction → Related Work → System Architecture and Generative Reasoning → Experimental Methodology → Empirical Results and Multi-Layer Evaluation → Conclusion → Disclosure Statement → References — and each section does clearly-scoped, distinct work. The System Architecture section (III) is particularly well-organized, walking the reader through the four-stage pipeline (telemetry ingestion, generative reasoning, impact self-assessment, fuzzy verification) before diving into the formal mathematics, which gives a reader unfamiliar with fuzzy control systems a clear conceptual scaffold before encountering the notation.

The mathematical formalization is precise and consistently notated throughout (x, I, φLLM, RX/CRC/BGP membership partitions, wk, R, R*), and the explicit rule-base examples (e.g., "IF CRC is Severo AND I is Leve THEN R is Bloquear") are a nice, concrete illustration of how the abstract fuzzy machinery translates into actual decision logic — this is good expository practice, making an otherwise dense mathematical section accessible. Tables I and II are clearly laid out and directly support the specific percentages cited in the prose, and Figures 1–2 (bar chart of decision counts, heatmap of per-scenario decision rates) are described and referenced appropriately in the text.

The prose itself is fluent, grammatically clean, and uses consistent technical vocabulary throughout (Approve/Supervise/Block terminology is stable from the System Architecture section through the Conclusion), with no jarring register shifts or evident seams. The paper does not suffer from the redundancy problems seen in some weaker submissions in this pool — each section adds distinct content rather than restating prior sections' claims.

The paper's presentation quality is undercut somewhat by its consistently promotional rhetorical register (discussed in Category 4) — phrases like "eliminates the catastrophic risks of unconstrained generative actuation" and "clear superiority" recur throughout the Results and Conclusion, giving the paper a marketing-adjacent tone more typical of a product pitch than a measured scientific report — and by the absence of any Limitations section, which is as much a structural gap (an expected component of a rigorous empirical paper's structure) as it is a content gap. This combination of otherwise solid organizational execution with a structurally incomplete and rhetorically overconfident overall shape keeps this category below the top of its range.

## Category 6: Discussion Potential for the Panel
**Score: 6/10**

This paper offers the panel a genuinely useful discussion opportunity centered on a specific, identifiable architectural flaw: the safety-critical self-assessment circularity (an LLM's own reported "action impact" score gating whether its own proposed action executes, with no independent verification of that self-report) is a clean, concrete, and pedagogically valuable case study in a subtle but important LLM-agent-safety failure mode. The panel could use this paper to discuss a broader and important principle for evaluating "neuro-symbolic" or "guardrailed" LLM architectures generally: a deterministic symbolic layer only provides a genuine safety guarantee if its inputs are independently verified rather than self-reported by the same untrusted component the guardrail is meant to constrain, and this paper is a useful, specific illustration of a design that superficially resembles a rigorous safety architecture (citing GuardAgent and VeriGuard as inspiration) while missing this crucial independence property that those very papers emphasize.

Second, the complete absence of a Limitations section, combined with uniformly triumphant results across every single comparison, is a useful contrastive case study for the panel when calibrated against the pool's most self-critical submissions (which report failed hypotheses, negative results, and explicit boundary conditions with equal prominence to their positive findings) — the panel could use this paper as a concrete example of what happens to a paper's persuasiveness and credibility when a plausible architectural idea is presented without any of the adversarial self-scrutiny that distinguishes the strongest submissions in this pool.

Third, the disclosure's relative vagueness about how the safety-critical experimental data (the LLM's self-reported action_impact scores across 600 synthetic events) was actually generated is a useful, concrete prompt for the panel to discuss what minimum level of experimental-provenance disclosure this track should require — specifically, whether a paper's central empirical claim should be considered adequately grounded when the disclosure does not specify whether the core measured quantity came from genuine model inference calls or some other process.

The paper's discussion value is real but comparatively narrower than the pool's strongest positive or negative exemplars — it does not offer the dramatic self-correction narratives, formally proven theorems, or explicitly falsified pre-registered hypotheses that make several other submissions rich live-discussion centerpieces, and its primary value to the panel is as a moderately instructive example of an under-scrutinized architectural design rather than as either an exemplary or a maximally cautionary case study.

## Summary Recommendation

This paper should **not** be ranked among the top 11 submissions. While it presents a coherent and reasonably well-motivated architectural idea — combining an LLM's semantic reasoning with a deterministic Mamdani fuzzy-logic safety gate for autonomous NetOps — the paper's central safety mechanism rests on an unaddressed circularity: the "action impact" score that gates whether an LLM-proposed action executes is self-reported by the very same LLM, with no independent verification against the actual content of the proposed mitigation, undermining the credibility of the entire safety architecture's core claim. This technical gap is compounded by a complete absence of any Limitations section, uniformly triumphant and unhedged reporting of results across every comparison with no acknowledged failure mode or negative finding, an experimental dataset whose generation process for the safety-critical impact scores is not adequately disclosed, and a disclosure statement that is markedly less specific and self-auditing than the strongest examples in this reviewed pool. While technically competent in its fuzzy-logic mathematics and reasonably well-organized in its prose, this paper falls well short of the standard of critical self-scrutiny, methodological rigor, and honest limitation-reporting demonstrated by the top-tier submissions in this batch.
