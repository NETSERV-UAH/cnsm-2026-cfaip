# TPC Review — CNSM 2026 "The Agentic AI Researcher" Track
## Paper: "SPEAR-5G: A Stackelberg Game-Theoretic Framework for Attack-Resilient Deployment of Non-Public 5G Networks"

---

## Category 1: Technical Correctness, Soundness & Red Flags
**Score: 19/25**

The game-theoretic formulation in this paper is technically well-constructed and internally consistent. The Stackelberg security-game setup — a leader (enterprise) committing to a mixed strategy σ over the simplex, observed by a follower (attacker) who best-responds with an effort level e — is a correct and standard application of the security-games framework pioneered by Tambe et al. The saturating-exponential success-probability model pj(e) = 1 − e^(−θj·e) is a reasonable, correctly-normalized diminishing-returns specification (p→0 as e→0, p→1 as e→∞, increasing in both effort and exposure), and the attacker's utility (Eq. 2) with a strictly convex quadratic effort cost c1·e² against a concave, saturating benefit term is correctly shown to yield a well-behaved concave optimization — the paper's claim that the best response is uniquely characterized by the first-order condition (Eq. 4), because the LHS is strictly decreasing in e past the origin, is mathematically sound and appropriately justifies the bisection-based numerical solution rather than asserting a closed form that doesn't exist.

The leader's problem (Eq. 5) is correctly posed as a bilevel program without closed-form solution — the paper is honest that σ*'s dependence on the implicitly-defined e*(σ) precludes an analytic solution — and the discretized simplex-search procedure (Algorithm 1) is a reasonable, if computationally brute-force, approach for a 4-dimensional simplex, correctly described as "analogous in spirit" to (not identical to) DOBSS rather than overclaiming equivalence to the established multiple-LP algorithm. The Bayesian extension in Section IV-C is a correct, minimal generalization (prior-weighted expectation over per-type best responses), consistent with the Paruchuri et al. Bayesian Stackelberg formulation it cites, and the paper is appropriately restrained in describing this as requiring "no change to Algorithm 1 beyond evaluating the inner loop once per type" rather than presenting it as a separate novel algorithm.

On the reference list, the citations are a mix of clearly real, well-known works (Tambe's *Security and Game Theory* monograph; Yin et al., JAIR 2010; Paruchuri et al.'s DOBSS paper, AAMAS 2008; Caballero et al., IEEE/ACM ToN 2019; Bitsikas et al., WiSec 2023) that are correctly attributed and topically on-point, alongside the paper's single foundational citation, Tripathi et al. [2] ("Security framework for non-public 5G network deployments," ICDCN '24), which the paper explicitly builds its entire cost taxonomy and attack model on. This citation is notably specific (short-paper pages 248–249, consistent with an ICDCN poster/short-paper format) and plausible, but — as with several other papers in this pool — the reviewer cannot independently verify it, and given that the entire paper's Table I taxonomy and attack model are stated to be adopted wholesale from this single source, its accuracy is unusually load-bearing; the panel should treat this as the one citation most worth spot-checking. No fabricated DOI pattern or obviously wrong venue/year mismatch was otherwise detected.

The paper's most important, and honestly disclosed, technical caveat is that the entire numerical evaluation rests on hand-calibrated, illustrative parameters (Table II) chosen only to be "qualitatively plausible" — there is no claim that Cj, θj, B, L, or c1 correspond to any measured quantity. This is a legitimate and significant soundness limitation (the specific 19% figure is an artifact of chosen constants, not an empirical finding), but the paper does not hide this; it is flagged repeatedly and explicitly, including in a dedicated Limitations subsection, which mitigates what would otherwise be a serious red flag into a well-disclosed scope restriction.

## Category 2: Novelty, Research Value & Core Strengths
**Score: 16/20**

The paper's central move — converting a static, cost-driven multi-objective optimization for non-public 5G deployment selection (Tripathi et al.) into a genuine two-player Stackelberg game where the attacker's effort is an endogenous best response rather than a fixed term — is a clean, well-motivated, and legitimate conceptual contribution. The critique of the baseline approach is specific and correct: folding "attacker cost" and "attacker benefit" into fixed feasibility-constraint terms (Cres+Cacc+Cap < Cue+Cnc+Cd) genuinely does ignore that a rational attacker would invest differently against different architectures, and the paper's fix — making success probability a function of both attacker effort and architecture-specific exposure θj — is the minimal, correct structural change needed to convert the static formulation into a proper game, exactly as the paper claims in Section III's "From static costs to a game" discussion.

The paper's most genuinely valuable and non-obvious research finding is the counter-intuitive result in Fig. 3/Section VI ("Effect of randomization on the attacker"): naive uniform randomization can *underperform* a deterministic static baseline at low attacker strength, because spreading probability mass onto high-exposure architectures can raise expected attack success even as per-architecture attacker effort falls. This is a substantive, well-reasoned insight that goes beyond "our optimized method wins" — it identifies and explains a specific failure mode of an intuitively-plausible alternative strategy (undirected randomization), which is exactly the kind of mechanism-level finding that gives a result genuine explanatory value rather than just a benchmark table entry. The paper correctly connects this to why the uniform-random baseline underperforms even the static baseline in Fig. 1 at low attacker strength — a specific, falsifiable causal claim tied directly to the model structure.

The application domain (non-public 5G deployment architecture selection) is a reasonably novel target for Stackelberg security games — the Related Work section correctly and specifically distinguishes this paper's contribution from the two adjacent literatures it must be checked against (game theory for 5G slicing/resource allocation, which targets cooperative/self-interested tenants rather than adversarial attackers; and cost-driven non-public 5G security, which lacks the strategic-attacker formulation) — and the "to our knowledge, no prior work..." novelty claim in Section II is specific and appropriately scoped rather than a sweeping generic assertion.

The paper's novelty ceiling, however, is real: the underlying machinery (SSE computation via simplex search, the Bayesian extension for attacker-type uncertainty, the saturating-exponential success model) is a direct, largely unmodified transplant of well-established 15-to-20-year-old security-games methodology (Tambe, Paruchuri et al., Yin et al.) into a new but fairly narrow application domain, with the core mathematical contribution being the specific payoff functions (Eq. 1–3) rather than any new equilibrium-computation technique. This is legitimate applied novelty — a genuine and useful contribution — but it is closer to "careful, correct domain transplant with one non-obvious empirical finding" than to a foundational methodological advance, and the disclosed absence of any real testbed validation means the practical impact claim (up to 19% cost reduction) cannot yet be taken as evidence about real non-public 5G deployments.

## Category 3: Adherence to Track Rules & Disclosure
**Score: 15/15**

The disclosure statement, while brief relative to some other submissions in this pool, is exceptionally clear, honest, and appropriately blunt about the paper's status. It explicitly states that "this entire manuscript" — title, framing, literature selection, mathematical formulation, algorithm, simulation code, figures, numerical results, and conclusions — was generated by an AI language model (Claude Sonnet 5) at a user's request, and it names the specific foundational source (Tripathi et al.) the model and taxonomy were built on. This satisfies the track's core disclosure requirement without ambiguity.

What distinguishes this disclosure is its unusually direct framing of epistemic status: it explicitly states the evaluation is "based on synthetic, hand-calibrated simulation data chosen to be qualitatively plausible, not on measurements from a real non-public 5G deployment or testbed," that the reference list was checked against web search "for bibliographic accuracy at generation time" but that "neither the technical contribution nor the results have been peer-reviewed or validated by a human domain expert," and — most notably — that "the paper should not be treated as a genuine scientific contribution, cited as prior work, or used as a basis for real deployment decisions without independent verification." This is a stronger and more self-limiting disclaimer than any other paper in this reviewed batch, and it correctly anticipates and forecloses exactly the kind of misuse (citing an unvalidated AI-generated paper as if it were peer-reviewed prior work) that the track's disclosure requirement is presumably designed to prevent.

The explicit statement that "no human author, affiliation, or institution is associated with this document" is also a valuable point of contrast with several other papers in this pool that attribute a submission to a named human author or research director while an AI performed the technical work — this paper takes the cleanest possible stance by disclaiming any human authorship claim whatsoever, removing any ambiguity about who bears responsibility for the content.

On AI-tone consistency, the technical core is uniform, precise, and stylistically coherent throughout — the register does not shift between the Introduction, the mathematical formulation, and the Discussion, and there are no template artifacts, dangling sentences, or other hygiene issues of the kind flagged in some other submissions in this pool. Combined with the exceptionally candid self-limiting disclaimer, this disclosure statement — despite being shorter than some others reviewed — fully and cleanly satisfies the track's transparency requirement.

## Category 4: Results, Evaluation, and Honesty on Limitations
**Score: 10/15**

The results are logically well-derived from the stated methodology: each of the four reported experiments (cost vs. attacker strength, equilibrium composition vs. risk-aversion, effect of randomization on attacker effort, and the cost-security Pareto frontier) follows directly and traceably from the model defined in Section IV, and the qualitative patterns reported are consistent with what the model structure would predict — e.g., the convergence of all three strategies' costs at very high attacker strength (since pj(e)→1 regardless of architecture once the attacker is sufficiently well-resourced) is exactly the correct asymptotic behavior implied by Eq. 1, and the paper correctly identifies and explains this rather than treating it as an unexplained artifact. The equilibrium-composition analysis (Fig. 2) is similarly well-reasoned: the paper explicitly notes it "reproduces, as a special case, the qualitative cost-vs-isolation trade-off discussed narratively in [2], but now derived as an equilibrium outcome rather than assumed" — this is a genuinely appropriate framing that neither overclaims novelty nor undersells the value of formally deriving a previously-only-narratively-argued pattern.

The central honesty concern for this category is that the paper's headline quantitative claim — "reducing the enterprise's total expected cost by up to 19%" — is prominently placed in the Abstract without an immediately adjacent caveat that this figure is entirely a function of the hand-chosen illustrative parameters in Table II, not an empirically grounded estimate. The Limitations section (Section VII, embedded within Discussion) does address this directly and honestly — "Our evaluation is simulation-based with illustrative, hand-calibrated parameters... not a substitute for measurements on a live testbed" — but this caveat arrives after the quantitative headline has already been stated with unqualified confidence in the Abstract and Results section. A more rigorous treatment would have hedged the 19% figure at first mention rather than only in the dedicated limitations discussion several pages later; this is a real, if moderate, instance of the paper's confident framing outrunning what a purely illustrative simulation can support.

The Limitations paragraph itself is substantive rather than boilerplate: it correctly flags that the exponential success-probability and quadratic effort-cost models, while standard in the security-games literature, are "unfit to empirical 5G attack-cost data," that the single-shot game abstracts away attacker learning across repeated interactions, and that the observability assumption (attacker observes the defender's full mixed strategy) is "a standard but non-trivial assumption inherited from the classical SSG literature" — each of these is a specific, accurate, and non-generic limitation rather than a reflexive hedge.

Unlike the strongest papers in this pool, this submission reports no negative or falsified findings of its own — every reported result favors SPEAR-5G over both baselines on every metric, which, combined with the absence of any confidence intervals, sensitivity analysis, or robustness check across alternative parameter choices (e.g., no sweep showing how the 19% figure changes under different Cj/θj/B/L calibrations), leaves the reader with less evidence than the strongest submissions in this pool that the reported advantage is robust rather than an artifact of one particular illustrative parameterization — a moderate but real gap relative to the standard the best papers in this batch set.

## Category 5: Structure, Clarity, and Formatting
**Score: 14/15**

The paper follows a clean, standard, and well-executed IEEE conference structure — Introduction → Related Work → System Model and Attack Model → Proposed Framework (players/strategies/payoffs, equilibrium, Bayesian extension) → Evaluation Methodology → Results → Discussion (with embedded Limitations) → Conclusion and Future Work — and each section builds coherently on the last, with the Introduction's three-item contributions list accurately previewing exactly the content that follows in Sections III–VI. The "From static costs to a game" subsection at the end of Section III is a particularly effective structural device: it explicitly bridges the prior-work cost taxonomy (Table I) to the new game-theoretic formulation, making the paper's conceptual contribution legible at exactly the point a reader needs it, rather than leaving the reader to infer the connection.

The mathematical exposition is precise and consistently notated (σ, e, θj, pj, UA, UD recur with stable meaning from Section IV through the Results), and Algorithm 1 is a clear, correctly-structured pseudocode listing that transparently describes the nested bisection-within-grid-search procedure without hidden steps. Table I (cost taxonomy) and Table II (simulation parameters) are well-designed and directly support the prose, and each of the four figures is described with a results paragraph that has a clear, bolded thematic header ("Cost vs. attacker strength," "Equilibrium composition vs. risk-aversion," "Effect of randomization on the attacker," "Cost–security Pareto frontier") that mirrors the effective claim-labeling style seen in the strongest paper in this batch, aiding a reader's ability to track the argument.

The Discussion section does solid synthesis work, correctly identifying three specific implications (non-uniform gain across attacker-strength regimes, randomization not being inherently protective, and the value of the Bayesian extension for hedging attacker-type uncertainty) rather than restating the results. The only minor clarity deduction is that the Limitations discussion is embedded as a short paragraph within the Discussion section rather than given its own clearly demarcated heading — a minor organizational choice that slightly reduces the visibility of the paper's caveats relative to papers that give limitations a dedicated, prominently-labeled section, which matters somewhat given the honesty concern raised in Category 4.

## Category 6: Discussion Potential for the Panel
**Score: 8/10**

This paper offers the panel a strong, distinct discussion angle centered on disclosure norms and self-limitation: its disclaimer is the most explicit in this pool about telling the reader not to treat the paper as a genuine scientific contribution or to cite it as prior work — a stance more self-effacing than any other submission reviewed. This raises a genuinely interesting question for the panel: should a track built around evaluating AI-generated research reward a paper more, or less, for stating this bluntly? One could argue this is exactly the epistemic honesty the track should want to encourage; one could equally argue that a paper explicitly telling readers it shouldn't be trusted as science is in tension with being a "submission" to a technical program at all. This tension is worth surfacing to the panel directly.

Second, the paper is a good illustration of a specific, common LLM behavior pattern worth discussing: taking a real, existing piece of published work (Tripathi et al.'s cost-driven formulation) and correctly identifying a genuine, well-known methodological gap relative to an adjacent, well-established literature (Stackelberg security games), then executing a technically clean but conceptually low-risk transplant. This is a useful case study for the panel to discuss what "novelty" should mean in this track — is correctly recognizing and filling a specific literature gap via established machinery sufficient, or does the track want to reward higher-risk, more original methodological invention (as seen in the null-operator or verification-gated-checkpointing papers in this pool)?

Third, the gap between the Abstract's confident "up to 19% lower total cost" framing and the Limitations section's much more hedged "illustrative, hand-calibrated parameters... not a substitute for measurements on a live testbed" is a concrete, specific example the panel could use to discuss where exactly the line falls between acceptable illustrative-simulation framing and overclaiming — a more subtle and arguably more common failure mode than outright fabrication, and a good calibration exercise for reviewers learning to spot it.

The paper is somewhat less dramatic as a panel centerpiece than the pool's strongest empirical or process-transparency case studies, since it contains no experimental red flags, no process incidents, and no self-falsified hypothesis — its discussion value is concentrated on disclosure philosophy and novelty calibration rather than on a dramatic methodological or integrity issue, which is real and useful but narrower in scope than the top case studies in this batch.

## Summary Recommendation

This paper presents a solid case for inclusion among the top 11 submissions, though not at the very top of the pool. It is technically sound — a correct, well-constructed Stackelberg security-game formulation with a legitimate closed-form best-response characterization and an honestly-scoped equilibrium-computation procedure — and it identifies a real, specific, and well-motivated gap in prior cost-driven non-public 5G security literature, surfacing at least one genuinely non-obvious finding (that naive randomization can underperform a deterministic baseline). Its disclosure statement is exemplary in its bluntness about the paper's non-peer-reviewed, illustrative-only status. Its main weaknesses are a comparatively narrow novelty ceiling (a careful but largely unmodified transplant of 15-to-20-year-old security-games machinery into a new domain), a headline quantitative claim (19% cost reduction) whose entirely illustrative parameterization is disclosed but not hedged at first mention, and the absence of any sensitivity analysis or negative/falsified finding of the kind that distinguishes the strongest submissions in this pool. It is a competent, honestly-framed, moderately novel contribution that merits a place in the middle-to-upper tier of the top 11 rather than being a leading contender for the top of the ranking.
