The simulation formulation isolates information quality cleanly by fixing the downstream policy across conditions and benchmarking against an oracle with perfect real-time state and model parameters, evaluating across three structurally distinct control problems: discrete scaling (DEC-A), admission control (DEC-B), and continuous load splitting (DEC-C).

**Category 2: Novelty, Research Value & Core Strengths**
Score: 19/20

The primary strength of this paper is its conceptual critique of the prevailing NDT synchronisation paradigm. While the telecommunications community has treated state synchronization error and Age of Information (AoI/AoDT) as standalone objectives, this paper questions whether state-space fidelity guarantees operational decision quality. By systematically bridging the NDT literature with goal-oriented and semantics-aware communications, the paper addresses an important blind spot in autonomous network management.

The discovery that a digital twin can exhibit exactly zero state error while inducing up to 54% action divergence from the oracle represents a genuine conceptual advance. The paper formalizes why this occurs: a digital twin is not merely a remote state estimator but an inductive forward model. If the parameterization of the forward model ($\hat{\theta}$) is miscalibrated, state synchronization at discrete telemetry intervals is insufficient to prevent flawed actuations.

The methodological approach—evaluating 97,200 seeded runs across a full-factorial grid of staleness, observation noise, model mismatch, and queue coverage—provides an empirical benchmark for the field. The negative results are particularly valuable: showing that a boundary-aware scheduler fails to outperform periodic sampling because belief drift corrupts distance-to-boundary estimation provides a clear cautionary lesson for future closed-loop telemetry scheduling designs.

**Category 4: Results, Evaluation, and Honesty on Limitations**
Score: 15/15

The empirical results are derived from the experimental methodology and supported by clear quantitative metrics, including normalized regret, action divergence, and bootstrap 95% confidence intervals. The evaluation covers 3,240 parameter configurations across 30 seeds, preventing small-sample biases and noise artifacts. The presentation in Figures 1–4 cleanly illustrates both the macro-level systematic review and the micro-level simulation dynamics.

The paper demonstrates exceptional scientific honesty. Rather than concealing negative outcomes, the authors emphasize that three of their five pre-registered hypotheses failed. Specifically, they report that regret does not follow a universal threshold-like slack region across all axes, that decision-type ordering for fidelity requirements is ill-posed across differing degradation vectors, and that their proposed boundary-aware scheduler (H4) failed to dominate periodic sampling.

Section VI details the threats to validity, frankly acknowledging the construct limitations of normalized regret, the synthetic nature of the vectorised queueing substrate, the absence of real physical testbeds or operator traffic traces, and the coding of paper abstracts in the systematic mapping. The paper avoids exaggerated claims, explicitly advising against transferring raw numerical thresholds to production deployments and framing its conclusions around the structural blindness of current fidelity metrics.
