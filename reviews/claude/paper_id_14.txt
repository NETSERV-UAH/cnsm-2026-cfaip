# TPC Review — CNSM 2026 "The Agentic AI Researcher" Track
## Paper: "Rename Before You Reconfigure: Metamorphic Quorums for Selective LLM NetOps"

---

## Category 1: Technical Correctness, Soundness & Red Flags
**Score: 24/25**

This paper's technical formulation is precise, correctly scoped, and — unusually for this pool — grounded in an exact, independently-audited oracle rather than an approximate or heuristic ground truth. The equivariance relation (Eq. 1) is correctly specified: πv(f(Tv(x))) = πb(f(Tb(x))) for all views v, with renaming explicitly handled as equivariant-under-bijection while the other five transforms are invariant after canonicalization — this is the technically correct way to define representation-equivariance for a planner, and the paper is careful to distinguish "a violation establishes representation dependence, not incorrectness; conversely, identical outputs can be unanimously unsafe," which is an important and easily-missed logical distinction that the paper gets exactly right rather than conflating consistency with correctness.

The six view transforms (base, rename, reorder, units, adjacency, paraphrase) are well-chosen and individually well-justified — deterministic, seed-derived, and validated by round-tripping every generated view back to the canonical public task before inference, which is a genuinely rigorous internal-consistency check that guards against the transform-generation process itself introducing errors that could be misattributed to the model under test. The three task families (failure rerouting, congestion remediation, edge migration) are specified with real networking precision — capacity constraints, lexicographic ranking by max post-change utilization then latency then hops for rerouting; CPU/latency-ceiling feasibility and a genuinely careful migration-time model using "the raw-capacity bottleneck (not residual capacity) of the widest among shortest-latency migration paths" for edge migration — and this level of specific, technically defensible tie-breaking logic (rather than a vague "best path" heuristic) reflects real domain competence.

The most impressive soundness feature of this paper is the independent oracle-verification step disclosed in Section VI: "The runner's exact oracle was checked by an independent uncapped implementation: it reconstructed all 60 optima, enumerated 543 routing paths (44 in the largest case), and exhaustively checked 1,836 ordered migration node pairs." This is a genuine, brute-force cross-validation of the paper's ground truth against a second, independently-built implementation — exactly the kind of oracle-integrity check that gives the paper's "exact" optimality/safety labels real evidentiary weight, rather than asking the reader to simply trust a single scoring implementation. Combined with the fail-closed analysis pipeline ("Analysis fails closed unless all expected calls, manifests, prompt hashes, and recomputed classifications match"), this is a technically serious, auditable measurement infrastructure.

On the reference list, this is one of the cleanest and most carefully verified bibliographies in the entire reviewed pool. Every entry is specific, dated, and directly verifiable: Ge et al. (graph descriptive order, ACL 2025, with a specific ACL Anthology URL), Chen et al. (metamorphic testing survey, ACM Computing Surveys 2018), Wang et al. (self-consistency, ICLR 2023, with an OpenReview link), Geifman & El-Yaniv (selective classification, NeurIPS 2017), Knight et al. (Internet Topology Zoo, IEEE JSAC 2011), and the three evaluated models' own technical reports (Qwen3, Llama 3 herd, Gemma 3) are all real, well-known, and correctly attributed. Several 2025–2026 arXiv preprints (Protogeros et al., Asadli et al., Thushalika et al., He & Yu, Zhou et al.) are cited with specific arXiv identifiers that the paper's own disclosure claims were checked "against primary metadata" — this reviewer cannot independently verify the very newest of these, but the pattern (correct venue/date pairing, plausible and internally consistent identifiers, precise page/section-level specificity in the in-text engagement with each source, e.g., correctly distinguishing Cornetto's evaluation focus from the follow-on agentic-repair paper) is highly consistent with genuine retrieval rather than fabrication, and no entry shows the telltale signs (wrong venue-year pairing, suspiciously round page numbers, mismatched author lists) that would flag a hallucinated citation.

## Category 2: Novelty, Research Value & Core Strengths
**Score: 19/20**

This paper identifies a genuinely sharp and underexplored question — not "can an LLM produce a correct network action," which is well-trodden ground (Cornetto, Asadli et al., explicitly cited and correctly distinguished from this paper's narrower scope), but "does an LLM planner produce the *same* action when the identical underlying network state is merely re-represented?" This reframing is conceptually elegant and directly consequential for exactly the agentic-NetOps deployment scenario this track cares about: if a planner's output depends on whether identifiers are renamed, records are reordered, or units are converted, that planner is unsafe to sit in front of a deterministic controller regardless of how well it scores on any single fixed representation — a risk that purely functional benchmarks (which test one fixed prompt format) cannot detect at all. This is a genuinely novel angle of attack on LLM-NetOps safety, distinct from and complementary to the configuration-repair literature it carefully positions itself against.

The paper's most valuable empirical contribution is the demonstration that marginal accuracy metrics can actively conceal instability: Cochran's Q tests show optimal/unsafe rates do *not* vary significantly across views (p = .938, .0677, .468) while exact action identity reveals violations on 58–60 of 60 tasks — meaning a researcher who only checked aggregate accuracy-by-view would conclude the model is robust, while the exact-match audit reveals it is almost never actually representation-invariant. This is a substantive, non-obvious methodological finding with implications well beyond this specific benchmark: it is a concrete demonstration that marginal/aggregate metrics are the wrong tool for measuring representation robustness, and that exact-match auditing at the level of individual actions is necessary to detect a real and consequential failure mode.

The structured-quorum (SQ6) mechanism itself is a clean, well-motivated design: using representational diversity (six different equivalent views) rather than only stochastic sampling diversity (SC6, six same-prompt draws) as the source of voting disagreement is a genuinely different and better-targeted diagnostic for exactly the failure mode the paper is studying, and the paper's explicit, correctly-caveated framing of the result — SQ6 does not "certify safety," it identifies disagreement and triggers abstention, and "correlated errors may agree" even under high quorum — is exactly the right epistemic humility for what a voting-based selective-prediction mechanism can actually deliver. The finding that SQ6 sharply reduces conditional unsafe-actuation rate for two of three models but not the third (Gemma's CI includes zero: −9.6 [−30.0, 11.4]) is reported as a genuine, model-dependent null result rather than smoothed over, which strengthens rather than weakens the paper's credibility.

The paper's novelty ceiling is that its underlying components (metamorphic testing, selective classification/abstention, self-consistency voting) are each well-established, and the paper is explicitly and repeatedly honest about this ("We do not claim to introduce metamorphic prompt variation, logical-invariance testing, or semantic quorums in general... Our contribution is the executable NetOps comparison and oracle-backed measurement, not the underlying notions"). This is an appropriately modest and accurate novelty claim rather than an overclaim, and the paper's actual contribution — a rigorously executed, oracle-verified instantiation of these ideas for a genuinely new and consequential problem (executable NetOps action equivariance) — is real and valuable even though it does not introduce fundamentally new machinery.

## Category 3: Adherence to Track Rules & Disclosure
**Score: 15/15**

The disclosure appendix is exceptionally detailed and transparent, and it includes a feature no other paper in this pool has: the *exact, verbatim* initial Research Director prompt, quoted directly rather than paraphrased or summarized. This prompt — casual, open-ended, explicitly telling the AI system "Pick anything at all that you think would have a good or great chance of getting accepted... I am actually curious myself what you will come up with" — is remarkable evidence of genuine topic autonomy: the human did not specify metamorphic testing, representation equivariance, or any technical direction at all, and the disclosure explicitly confirms this ("supplied no technical proposal, result, or hidden reviewer instruction for this manuscript... The Research Director did not supply or manually edit this paper's topic, method, code, data, results, references, structure, or prose"). This is a genuinely strong, directly verifiable demonstration of autonomous research-topic selection, which is precisely the kind of evidence this track should most want to see and reward.

The disclosure is also careful to draw a clean, explicit boundary around what the three evaluated LLMs (Qwen3-8B, Llama3.1-8B, Gemma3-4B) actually did versus what the authoring system (GPT-5.6 Sol) did: "They did not author the research question, experiment code, analysis, or manuscript... separating them from the Codex authoring system." This is an important and easily-blurred distinction — the paper studies three models as experimental subjects while being written by a fourth, different system — and the disclosure's explicit separation of these roles prevents any ambiguity about which AI did what.

The disclosure additionally documents a methodologically important detail with complete honesty: a pilot study ("a materially different 12-scenario/60-call Qwen pilot") preceded the frozen protocol, tested only feasibility rather than hypotheses, and is explicitly excluded from the reported dataset — while the disclosure candidly notes "its existence may have influenced engineering choices even though none of its outcomes enters the reported analysis." This kind of proactive acknowledgment of a subtle, hard-to-fully-eliminate form of information leakage (engineering choices informed by an excluded pilot) is a mark of unusually careful self-audit, on par with the strongest disclosure practices seen elsewhere in this pool.

The technical core reads with complete stylistic consistency from Introduction through Conclusion, with dense, precise, appropriately hedged prose throughout and no discernible seams. Combined with a fully specified reproducibility artifact (a hosted URL for frozen scenarios, code, raw records, manifests, and analysis outputs; a SHA-256 hash for the JSONL benchmark; full model digest prefixes), this disclosure fully and cleanly satisfies — and in the verbatim-prompt-quotation respect, exceeds — the track's transparency requirements.

## Category 4: Results, Evaluation, and Honesty on Limitations
**Score: 15/15**

This paper's results are reported with exceptional statistical discipline and honest, non-inflated interpretation throughout. The central quantitative claims are precise and directly traceable to Table I and Fig. 2: at q=4, unsafe/all-case counts fell from 25→3 (Qwen), 22→1 (Llama), and 34→8 (Gemma), with exact paired McNemar tests remaining significant after cross-model Holm adjustment (padj < 4×10⁻⁶ for all three) — but the paper immediately and correctly attributes the mechanism to abstention rather than improved planning: "The mechanism was selective: commitments fell from 59 to 16, 38 to 8, and 60 to 17... Optimal/all-case counts fell by 3, 3, and 2 (padj = .75 each); optimal throughput did not improve." This is a critical, easily-elided distinction that the paper foregrounds rather than burying: SQ6 does not make the model better at finding the right answer, it makes the system better at knowing when *not* to act, and the paper states this difference explicitly and repeatedly rather than letting the headline unsafe-rate reduction imply an accuracy improvement it did not actually achieve.

The paper's honesty extends to reporting a genuine model-dependent null result without minimizing it: the SQ6-minus-SC6 conditional-risk difference for Gemma has a bootstrap 95% CI of [−30.0, 11.4], which includes zero, and the paper states plainly "conditional risk clearly fell for Qwen and Llama, but not for Gemma" — this is exactly the correct statistical caution (not claiming an effect where the interval doesn't support one) and stands in sharp contrast to papers in this pool that report only aggregated, uniformly-favorable outcomes. The coverage-cost tradeoff is also reported with complete transparency: SQ6 achieves its safety benefit at a real cost (coverage falling to 13.3–28.3%, meaning the vast majority of cases are abstained on rather than acted on) and a real compute cost (11.2–13.5% more prompt tokens, 2.8–44.7% more wall time), both explicitly quantified per-model rather than glossed as a footnote.

Section VI's "Interpretation and Threats to Validity" is one of the most rigorous and well-organized limitations discussions in this reviewed pool, explicitly partitioned into Construct/Internal/External validity with substantive, specific content in each: construct validity correctly notes the benchmark omits "protocol convergence, stale telemetry, concurrent actions, access control, rollback, packet-level timing, and device semantics," and explicitly flags that "paraphrase equivalence is weaker than the mechanically invertible transformations" — a genuine, specific admission that one of the paper's own six views rests on a less rigorous equivalence relation than the other five. The paper also explicitly warns against over-reading its own sparse-endpoint result ("SQ6 made only two, zero, and zero commitments at q=6; observing no unsafe SQ6 commitment at that sparse endpoint is not a guarantee") — proactively heading off exactly the kind of overclaim a less careful paper would be tempted to make from a flattering but statistically thin data point.

The Conclusion is a model of calibrated, non-triumphalist synthesis: "structured views exposed representation dependence and sharply reduced unsafe-actuation incidence at q=4, mainly through abstention; they neither improved optimal throughput nor guaranteed safety... This frozen-suite result characterizes an abstaining heuristic, not a deployment guarantee." This precise, hedge-appropriate final framing — refusing to claim more than the evidence supports even in the paper's own closing statement — exemplifies exactly the standard of honesty this rubric is designed to reward, and it does so as thoroughly and consistently as any paper in this pool.

## Category 5: Structure, Clarity, and Formatting
**Score: 14/15**

The paper follows a rigorous, logically sequenced structure — Introduction (with a clearly delineated three-item contribution list) → Background and Related Work → Metamorphic Quorums (equivalence relation, selective voting policies) → Benchmark and Experimental Protocol (tasks, models, metrics/statistics) → Results → Interpretation and Threats to Validity → Conclusion → Appendix (Disclosure) — and each section builds precisely on established notation without redundant restatement. The Related Work section is unusually well-executed for a short paper: it doesn't just list adjacent work but explicitly and specifically differentiates this paper's contribution from each — correctly noting, for instance, that Semantic Quorum Assurance's heterogeneous validator panel is "much narrower" here (one planner, six views, unweighted vote) than He & Yu's signed, weighted, risk-adaptive approval predicate with vetoes — which gives the novelty claim in Category 2 real, checkable substantiation directly in the text.

The mathematical notation (x, f, Tv, πv, the equivariance relation, cR(a), q) is used precisely and consistently from Section III through the Results, and Table I and Figures 1–2 are well-designed and directly support the prose without redundant duplication — Fig. 1's per-view optimal/unsafe marginal rates and Fig. 2's coverage-vs-conditional-risk threshold sweep are complementary and each earns its place by revealing something the other does not (Fig. 1 shows marginal-rate stability masking instability; Fig. 2 shows the actual coverage-risk tradeoff curve across all six thresholds). The explicit numerical grounding throughout (precise call counts, token counts, wall-clock times, p-values, confidence intervals reported to one decimal place) gives the paper a level of internal verifiability matched by very few other submissions in this pool.

The prose is dense and highly information-rich — this is unmistakably a paper packed with methodological and statistical content within a tight five-page limit — which occasionally demands careful re-reading (e.g., the exact distinction between "commit" and "all-case" denominators in Table I's columns requires attention to fully parse on first pass), but this density is a reasonable and even appropriate cost of genuine rigor rather than a clarity failure per se. The paper's title and framing ("Rename Before You Reconfigure") is a clean, memorable, and technically accurate encapsulation of its core finding, and the overall writing register remains precise and appropriately technical throughout without the whimsical or metaphor-heavy style of the companion Bober-Clos submission from the same authoring lineage — a deliberate and fitting choice for a paper whose entire point is about precision and representational fidelity.

## Category 6: Discussion Potential for the Panel
**Score: 10/10**

This paper offers the panel an outstanding and multifaceted discussion opportunity. First and most directly, its verbatim-quoted Research Director prompt is a uniquely strong, directly auditable piece of evidence for genuine topic autonomy in agentic research: the human's instruction was explicitly open-ended ("Pick anything at all... I am actually curious myself what you will come up with"), and the panel can read this prompt themselves and judge firsthand whether the resulting paper represents authentic independent topic selection — this is a rare case in the pool where the disclosure doesn't just *claim* autonomy but *shows the exact evidence* a reader needs to judge the claim independently, making it an ideal anchor for a live discussion about what genuine agentic research initiative looks like.

Second, the paper's central empirical finding — that marginal accuracy/safety rates can be statistically indistinguishable across representations (Cochran's Q, p > .06 for all three models) while exact action identity reveals near-universal instability (58–60 of 60 tasks) — is a genuinely important and debate-worthy methodological point with implications reaching well beyond this specific paper: it is a concrete, quantified demonstration that current LLM-NetOps evaluation practice, if it relies on aggregate accuracy metrics rather than exact-match cross-representation audits, could be systematically blind to a real and serious deployment risk. The panel could productively debate whether this finding should change how the *track itself* evaluates other LLM-NetOps submissions in this pool.

Third, this paper's companion relationship to the Bober-Clos submission from the same disclosed authoring lineage (both list "Castor Codex" and "Rob White" as authors, and this paper's disclosure explicitly references "a separate satirical Bober-paper project" in its inherited conversation context) gives the panel a rich, direct opportunity to compare two papers produced by extremely similar agentic pipelines under different topic constraints — one deliberately satirical-but-rigorous, one soberly technical-and-rigorous — as a case study in how consistent the underlying research discipline (ablations, honest limitations, calibrated claims) remains across very different presentational registers from what appears to be the same underlying agent configuration.

Fourth, the paper's rigorous, independently-audited oracle (reconstructing all 60 optima via a separate uncapped implementation) is a strong, concrete example of an agentic system applying genuine adversarial self-scrutiny to its own ground truth before trusting any downstream result — a practice the panel could hold up as exemplary and worth debating as a possible requirement for future submissions to this track.

## Summary Recommendation

This paper should be ranked at or very near the top of the top 11 submissions. It identifies a sharp, genuinely underexplored, and directly consequential question for agentic NetOps — whether an LLM planner's action changes under semantically equivalent but syntactically different representations of the same network state — and answers it with an exceptionally rigorous, oracle-verified, statistically disciplined empirical study: an independently cross-validated ground truth, fail-closed analysis infrastructure, appropriately hedged causal claims (correctly attributing the safety improvement to abstention rather than better planning), an honest model-dependent null result (Gemma), and one of the most thorough and well-organized threats-to-validity discussions in the entire pool. Its disclosure statement is exceptional, featuring a verbatim, directly auditable Research Director prompt that offers unusually strong, checkable evidence of genuine topic autonomy. Combined with a clean, well-verified reference list and a precise, technically confident writing style throughout, this paper is a leading candidate for the single best submission in this pool, on par with or exceeding the strongest empirical paper reviewed elsewhere in this batch.
