## Category 1: Technical Correctness, Soundness & Red Flags — **Score: 19/25**

The experimental foundation is technically strong and unusually concrete for this track. The paper evaluates actual model artifacts on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin rather than extrapolating server measurements to an edge platform, fixes the llama.cpp revision, records artifact hashes, uses identical 1,197-question subsets per model, scores the literal outputs produced by each artifact, and measures energy request-by-request.  The energy methodology is also defensible: NVIDIA documents `VDD_GPU_SOC`, `VDD_CPU_CV`, and `VIN_SYS_5V0` as distinct AGX Orin monitored power domains, so summing those three rails does not obviously double-count the same power. ([NVIDIA Docs][1]) The use of gross and idle-subtracted net energy is particularly useful because it makes explicit that an always-on board and a battery-powered intermittent system have different optimization objectives.

There are, however, several technical and interpretative red flags. Most importantly, the headline that the server benchmark does not identify the best edge deployment is partly a **roster-expansion effect rather than a server-to-edge ranking reversal**. LFM2-8B-A1B, which becomes the Jetson winner, was absent from the source study's published ranking; within the original roster, Qwen2.5-7B remains the accuracy winner and LFM2-350M remains the published Edge-Score winner on both systems.  The paper acknowledges this, but it substantially narrows the central conclusion. There are also two concrete internal inconsistencies. Section IV-C says Qwen2.5-7B Q4 increases median latency from 7.40 s to 8.41 s, then states that the same Q4 conversion saves memory and energy “without changing accuracy or latency.”  Later, the power-mode discussion says MAXN's 1.21 s is versus **4.17 s at 15 W**, whereas Figure 2 on page 4 shows approximately 7.9 s at 15 W and 4.2 s at 30 W.  These are editorial errors rather than fatal experimental flaws, but an autonomous end-to-end checking pipeline should catch them.

The reference list is mostly credible, but it contains at least one verifiable metadata error. Core references such as *How Small Can 6G Reason?*, NetIntent, the Jetson edge-accelerator study, and TeleQnA are genuine; the original 6G study indeed used eight A100 GPUs and defined Edge Score as accuracy divided by latency and memory. ([arXiv][2]) TeleQnA's DOI 10.1109/MNET.2025.3576035 is also correct, with its archival issue appearing in *IEEE Network* 40(2), 2026. ([DOI][3]) Reference [14], however, calls NetSoft 2026 the **12th** International Conference on Network Softwarization, whereas the authors' repository and Politecnico di Milano record identify it as the **11th** edition; the DOI itself appears genuine. ([GitHub][4]) This is a small but real citation-quality failure rather than evidence of wholesale bibliographic hallucination.

## Category 2: Novelty, Research Value & Core Strengths — **Score: 17/20**

The real strength is the paper's direct investigation of an important but often hand-waved deployment assumption: whether model rankings obtained on server GPUs remain useful when the actual deployment target is a constrained edge platform. The paper goes beyond merely timing one model on a Jetson by reproducing a telecom-specific server ranking, deploying a fairly broad model/quantization roster, testing two telecommunications benchmarks, measuring energy, and examining precision and power-mode effects.  This is well aligned with practical 5G/6G management because model choice is ultimately a systems decision involving capability, latency, memory, energy, runtime, and prompt behavior, not simply parameter count.

The novelty is primarily **experimental systems novelty**, not methodological invention. Quantization benchmarking, Jetson power profiling, edge LLM inference, and accuracy–latency–memory trade-offs have all been studied previously. The paper itself correctly cites prior Jetson work showing that lower precision can even be slower, and contemporary edge-accelerator research independently reports non-monotonic quantization behavior. ([arXiv][5]) What appears genuinely valuable is applying this question to telecom-specific workloads while linking the measurements directly back to published server rankings. The finding that rank correlation can remain high while particular artifact/runtime combinations collapse—for example Llama-3.2-1B answering essentially one option across four precisions—is more useful than a generic claim that “edge is slower.” 

The paper also contributes a useful deployment lesson: **benchmark the deployment artifact, not merely the model name**. The exact GGUF artifact, quantization, runtime commit, chat template, output parser, power mode, prompt length, and energy-accounting boundary can materially affect the result.  The strongest version of the paper's novelty claim should therefore not be “server ranking fails,” because the original-roster winners actually transfer reasonably well; it should be that server results are effective for **shortlisting**, while final deployment certification must occur on the target hardware/software stack. That narrower claim is both better supported and operationally valuable.

## Category 3: Adherence to Track Rules & Disclosure — **Score: 12/15**

The paper satisfies most formal requirements. It is exactly five pages including references and the disclosure, uses a conventional IEEE two-column presentation, and falls clearly within the allowed 5G/6G Networks and Edge Computing Services / Generative-AI-for-NetOps scope. The title page also transparently identifies it as AI-generated while naming the human Research Director context.  The official CFP requires a maximum of five pages and allows humans to select/constrain topics and re-initiate the agentic loop, while manual technical editing should be avoided. ([CNSM Conference][6])

The disclosure identifies Claude Fable 5 as the primary AI researcher, Claude Opus 5 and Sonnet 5 as secondary systems, and GPT-5.6 Sol/Codex as an AI reviewer and reviser. It also describes the Jetson resource provided to the system and says that Claude Code designed and executed the experiments after the Research Director selected one of several AI-generated research directions.  The technical prose is stylistically consistent throughout; I see no obvious section that looks manually inserted by a human. Human intermediate direction “through prompting” is not inherently non-compliant because the track expressly allows humans to re-initiate loops through prompting. ([CNSM Conference][6])

The main compliance deficiency is the **initial-prompt disclosure**. CNSM specifically requests the LLM(s), the exact initial prompting strategy, experiment/code framework, and constraints. ([CNSM Conference][6]) Here the appendix merely says that the initial prompt specified the broad topic and asked the AI to survey literature and identify gaps; it does not reproduce the exact initial instruction, detailed constraints, or later prompts.  The disclosure also provides less experiment-framework provenance than the strongest submissions in this set—for example no immutable prompt transcript/hash or full human-action ledger is identified. This is a meaningful but not disqualifying deduction because the models and broad human/AI roles are otherwise clearly stated.

## Category 4: Results, Evaluation, and Honesty on Limitations — **Score: 14/15**

The evaluation is strong. Each main model sees the same 1,197 scored questions per benchmark, Wilson intervals are reported for accuracy, server-versus-device rank transfer uses Spearman and Kendall statistics, and the key LFM2-versus-Qwen comparison uses an exact paired McNemar test rather than comparing two independent percentages.   The results are also multidimensional: actual output correctness, p50/p95 latency, process-memory high-water mark, gross and net energy, quantization inversions, and power-mode effects are measured. Figure 1 on page 4 is particularly useful because it visually shows both the high overall accuracy correlation and the Llama runtime collapse, preventing the headline correlation from hiding important outliers.

The authors are unusually willing to preserve findings that weaken their own preregistered expectations. C-E1 is reported only partly supported because Edge-Score transfer is stronger than expected; the hypothesized 1.5–3B accuracy-per-joule optimum is rejected after the authors recognize that a raw efficiency metric perversely rewards a nearly unusable 135M model; and a previous post-data filter that produced the desired optimum is explicitly identified as a mistake and replaced with a capability gate.   The strict-parser correction for TeleQnA is similarly transparent. This is strong autonomous scientific behavior: the agent identifies benchmark/parser pathologies and revises the interpretation rather than concealing them.

The remaining limitations are real but well acknowledged. There is only one Jetson board, each configuration is run once, the energy monitor samples at 20 Hz—fairly coarse for TeleQnA requests that can finish in a few tenths of a second—and no wall/battery-terminal meter independently validates the rail measurements.  The server/Jetson comparison also confounds hardware with precision/runtime for most configurations, and only four matched F16/BF16 cases partly isolate this.  The paper explicitly acknowledges those problems and avoids claiming that its Jetson results represent every edge platform. I therefore view them as limits on external validity rather than evidence of dishonest evaluation.

## Category 5: Structure, Clarity, and Formatting — **Score: 14/15**

The paper is very well organized. It moves efficiently from the deployment problem to prior evidence and pre-registered expectations, experimental apparatus, rank-transfer results, artifact/runtime anomalies, quantization, energy, unified-memory behavior, deployment procedure, limitations, conclusion, disclosure, and references. The Abstract is especially effective because it reports both the strong transfer result (\(\rho=0.92\)) and the counterexamples, rather than framing the study as a simplistic demonstration that server results are useless. 

The tables and figures are unusually information-dense but useful. Table I permits direct inspection of accuracy, latency, gross/net energy, and memory across the principal configurations; Table II separates rank-transfer analyses and explicitly shows how removing the unpublished-roster MoE model changes top-choice agreement.  Figure 2 on page 4 is also effective because it shows why “lower power mode” and “lower energy per query” are not synonymous: finishing faster at MAXN can lower gross energy despite higher instantaneous power. The explicit distinction between net and gross accounting is one of the paper's clearest presentation strengths.

The one-point deduction is for internal quality-control lapses that a final automated consistency pass should have caught. The Q4 paragraph simultaneously reports a 7.40-to-8.41 s latency increase and then describes latency as unchanged, while the power-mode paragraph substitutes the 30 W latency (4.17 s) for the 15 W latency despite Figure 2 showing approximately 7.9 s at 15 W.   Neither error changes the core result, but both matter in a track specifically testing whether an AI can autonomously produce a submission-ready artifact.

## Category 6: Discussion Potential for the Panel — **Score: 10/10**

This paper is an excellent panel case because it demonstrates an AI executing **real hardware experimentation**, not merely synthetic simulation or literature synthesis. The autonomous workflow deployed numerous artifacts on an actual Jetson, processed thousands of telecom questions per configuration, measured power rails, preserved raw outputs and traces, ran statistical comparisons, and revised earlier analyses after discovering scoring and filtering problems.  That substantially raises the bar for what “AI researcher” means relative to papers whose empirical worlds are entirely self-designed simulations.

It also produces a productive anti-hype result. The server benchmark is neither useless nor sufficient: accuracy rankings transfer surprisingly well, and the original server winner remains the winner within the original model roster, yet individual runtime/artifact failures and new architectures can change deployment decisions.  This nuanced answer is more scientifically interesting than an AI conveniently proving that its proposed edge benchmark invalidates all prior work. The paper also surfaces an important systems lesson for Agentic AI: a model identifier is not a deployable object; artifact conversion, quantization kernels, runtime, template, parser, workload, and power accounting are part of the behavior being certified.

The panel should nevertheless challenge the AI's framing choices. Introducing an MoE model absent from the published server ranking and then using it to say the server winner is not the best edge deployment risks conflating **ranking transfer** with **candidate-set expansion**. The paper itself contains the evidence needed to expose that nuance, which is a positive sign, but does not fully adjust its headline rhetoric. Similarly, the two internal numerical contradictions show that an autonomous system capable of sophisticated hardware experimentation and statistical self-correction can still miss straightforward cross-document consistency errors. That combination—high research competence with imperfect global editorial checking—is exactly the kind of capability boundary this track should debate.

# FINAL SCORE: **86/100**

**Top-11 recommendation:** **Yes.** I would rank this paper among the top 11 submissions, though below the very strongest artifacts such as those combining equally strong experimentation with formal assurance and cleaner provenance. Its real-hardware evaluation, telecom-specific workloads, measured per-request energy, strong artifact/runtime reproducibility, pre-registered hypotheses, statistical comparisons, and unusually candid correction of parser/filter mistakes constitute substantial research value. The main deductions come from incomplete exact-prompt disclosure, the partial confounding of hardware transfer with candidate-set/precision/runtime changes, one concrete bibliographic metadata error, and several internal prose/figure inconsistencies. These issues weaken the presentation but do not outweigh what is one of the more convincing autonomous **experimental systems** papers in the set.
