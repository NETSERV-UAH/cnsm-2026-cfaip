# TPC Review — CNSM 2026 "The Agentic AI Researcher" Track
## Paper: "When Does LLM Control Pay Off? A Volatility–Cadence Regime Map for Edge Inference Management"

---

## Category 1: Technical Correctness, Soundness & Red Flags
**Score: 25/25**

This paper is technically exceptional, and its central methodological achievement is a multi-stage adversarial disconfirmation of its own most flattering finding, executed with complete rigor. The registered utility function (U = −0.5Voff − 0.2A − 0.3C) is a sensible, correctly-weighted composite of SLO violation, accuracy deficit, and resource cost, and the paper's decision to compute a parallel "completed-only" variant (Uv2) alongside the primary metric, both pre-registered, is good methodological practice against metric-selection bias. The discrete-event simulation is grounded in real hardware measurements (single-stream p50 latencies and accuracy figures for three actual model variants measured on the Jetson), and the paper is careful to note a genuinely interesting empirical wrinkle discovered during calibration — that lite-1B is "a useful trap for the controller: it is less accurate than tiny-1B4 and no faster" — which is exactly the kind of adversarial-design detail that makes a benchmark actually discriminating rather than trivially easy.

The paper's headline result — that calibrated rules beat LLM controllers in 19 of 21 cells, with the two apparent LLM wins subsequently reversed — is built through a genuinely rigorous multi-stage falsification chain, and each stage is technically sound: (1) utility decomposition correctly reveals that Luna's apparent wins come from "lower resource cost and higher model accuracy" rather than better SLO management — the SLO component is explicitly *worse* in both winning cells; (2) proper Bonferroni correction across 21 comparisons is applied and correctly eliminates one of the two nominally significant wins (60s result drops from apparent significance to p=0.064); (3) an adversarial audit constructs a stronger, volatility-informed static baseline and demonstrates it beats Luna in both remaining cells, with a specific, mechanistically correct explanation (bursts exceeding all feasible capacity 8.8–9.8% of the time, meaning "extra capacity cannot recover these bursts, so minimal capacity with the most accurate model is the better policy"). This is genuine, technically sound adversarial science — not merely claimed rigor, but rigor that is demonstrably executed and whose mechanism is correctly explained at each stage.

The energy-measurement methodology in RQ3 is technically careful and appropriately humble about its own hardware limitations: the paper correctly notes "the Jetson exposes GPU and SoC power on a fused rail, so per-process attribution is not possible," cites a specific supporting reference for this hardware limitation, and instead reports idle-subtracted GPU+SoC energy per event — a methodologically sound workaround for a genuine measurement constraint rather than an unacknowledged gap. The explicit correction of a "2.5% idle-power artifact" discovered during audit, with the resulting energy-ratio range revised from a lower to a corrected estimate (1.9–12.1×) "without changing which three configurations exceed tenfold," is a specific, quantified, and consequential self-correction handled with complete transparency.

The reference list is compact (8 entries) but every citation is specific, topically precise, and directly load-bearing in the paper's argument — ORACL (chain-of-thought autoscaling, arXiv 2602.05292 2026), E3-Agent (edge inference resource management, arXiv 2605.27428 2026), the intent-driven orchestration paper (arXiv 2601.13694 2026), the adaptive-control-baseline-calibration paper (arXiv 2605.26418 2026) that directly motivates this paper's entire methodological premise, the classical MMPP burstiness paper (Mi et al., ICAC 2009), the Jetson energy-attribution limitation paper (arXiv 2605.27599 2026), the edge-LLM energy methodology paper (arXiv 2506.09554 2025), and the EcoMLS MAPE-K energy baseline (ICSA-C 2024). Each citation is engaged with specifically in the text (not just listed), and the paper's explicit "Relation to Prior Work" subsection ties each reference to a precise methodological role in this paper's design — this is a small, tight, and evidently carefully-curated reference list with no detectable fabrication pattern.

## Category 2: Novelty, Research Value & Core Strengths
**Score: 20/20**

This paper's core contribution is exactly the kind of rigorously executed negative/qualified result this rubric should reward most highly, and its framing is genuinely novel: rather than asking "can an LLM control this system well," it asks the much sharper, cost-aware question "does the LLM's decision quality improvement exceed its own operating cost, relative to *properly calibrated* alternatives" — explicitly motivated by citing prior work showing that "careful calibration alone can remove much of a reported learning advantage." This reframing directly targets a real and underappreciated methodological weakness across the broader LLM-for-systems-control literature (comparing learned/LLM controllers against default-configured, undertuned baselines), and the paper's entire experimental design is built to close exactly this gap rather than merely gesturing at it.

The paper's most valuable and distinctive contribution is the "disappearing effect" narrative itself, laid out explicitly in Table IV ("What the main observations do and do not establish") and the Discussion: Luna appears to win two cells against mixed-volatility-calibrated rules; utility decomposition reveals these are not genuine SLO wins; Bonferroni correction removes one; and a workload-informed static baseline removes the other. This staged-disconfirmation structure is a genuinely important methodological template — it demonstrates concretely *how* a plausible, statistically-flagged LLM-control advantage can be constructed by insufficiently strong baselines, and it gives the reader a transferable diagnostic procedure (decompose the utility, correct for multiple comparisons, and stress-test against a stronger baseline informed by the same information the LLM receives) rather than just a single result.

The intent-completeness experiments (Section IV) deliver an independently valuable and non-obvious finding: removing only the cost clause from the LLM's objective causes accuracy-maximizing over-provisioning (mean slots rising from 2.20 to 6.01, a 2.7× effect) while "the prompt remains fluent and valid" — meaning a natural-language interface can silently mask an incomplete specification in a way a parameter-based interface cannot. This is a genuinely important, practically-relevant safety observation about natural-language control interfaces specifically (not a generic "prompting matters" platitude), and the paper's derived recommendation — "intent validation should therefore check objective completeness before deployment, rather than rely on the model to infer missing costs" — is concrete and actionable.

The reproducibility findings (RQ2) are similarly valuable: the clean, mechanism-level distinction between byte-identical reproduction and decision-identical reproduction (local serial decoding achieves both; continuous batching preserves decisions but not exact wording; hosted models achieve neither reliably, with the lowest stability specifically "near a decision boundary, where inconsistent choices matter most") is a genuinely useful, transferable finding for anyone deploying an LLM controller in a safety- or SLO-relevant loop, and the concrete anecdote that "one byte-identical state even produced both a scale-up and a scale-down decision across repeats" is a vivid, memorable, and technically important illustration of hosted-model nondeterminism risk. Combined with the RQ3 energy quantification (14.1 J per deliberation, equivalent to 2–12 served requests, adding 1.6–9.3% overhead at realistic cadence) — a concrete, previously largely unmeasured cost quantity the paper explicitly identifies as needed for exactly the cost-benefit tests that motivated papers like E3-Agent lack — this paper delivers multiple independent, mechanism-level, actionable contributions rather than resting on a single result.

## Category 3: Adherence to Track Rules & Disclosure
**Score: 13/15**

The disclosure statement covers the essential required elements: it names the primary AI system (Claude Fable 5 via Claude Code, with Claude Opus 5 and Claude Sonnet 5 used to a lesser extent, and GPT-5.6 Sol via Codex for manuscript readability revision), describes the initial human prompt ("5G/6G Networks and Edge Computing Services," survey literature and identify gaps), and states that Claude Code "surveyed the literature and proposed several candidate research directions, from which the human Research Director selected one," after which it "conducted the experiments, analyzed the results, and drafted and revised the manuscript." This gives a clear, if not maximally granular, account of the human/AI division of labor, and — as with the companion paper from the same authorship group in this pool — the disclosure is honest that "the human Research Director monitored progress and provided some intermediate direction through prompting the AI," rather than claiming zero human involvement.

The paper's in-text methodological self-audit disclosures are a genuine strength that partially compensates for the appendix's relative brevity: Section IX explicitly states "before publication, fresh-context agents were instructed to challenge every headline result. Those audits found the stronger static baseline, the frozen local controller, a 2.5% idle-power artifact, and two self-triggered specification gaps. Each issue was recorded rather than silently repaired." This is a specific, itemized, and — critically — *verifiable against the paper's own content* account of adversarial review: each of these four audit findings is indeed visible and discussed in the paper's main results, giving the disclosure real substantiating evidence rather than an unverifiable claim.

As with the companion submission by the same named authors in this pool, this paper lists two real, named human co-authors (a graduate student and a senior, identifiable faculty member at the University of Toronto) on the byline, with the disclosure clarifying their role as prompting and progress monitoring rather than technical execution. This is a consistent and, by this point in the pool, recognizable authorship convention from this research group, and the panel should again weigh explicitly how much credit belongs to the AI system's autonomous execution versus the named humans' oversight, direction-selection, and iteration — a distinct and legitimate discussion point given this differs from the "fully AI-attributed" convention used by several other papers in this pool.

The technical core is stylistically consistent, dense, and precise throughout with no evident seams, and the disclosure statement — while adequate and specific about model identities, prompting origin, and the adversarial-audit practice — does not reach the maximal standard of hash-verified, itemized process transparency (verbatim prompt logs, timestamped ledgers, SHA-256-referenced provenance artifacts) demonstrated by the very strongest disclosure statements in this pool, which is the basis for a score just short of the maximum in this category.

## Category 4: Results, Evaluation, and Honesty on Limitations
**Score: 15/15**

This paper sets an exceptionally high standard for results honesty, and its entire narrative arc is structured around progressively disconfirming its own most favorable finding rather than showcasing it. The paper does not merely report that the LLM's two apparent wins don't survive scrutiny — it walks the reader through *why*, in the correct order, exactly reproducing the logic a skeptical reviewer would apply: first, decomposition reveals the wins are not SLO improvements; second, proper multiple-comparison correction removes one of the two; third, a legitimately stronger baseline removes the other. The paper's own Discussion explicitly frames this as pedagogically important: "This sequence matters because each stage answers a different question," which is a sophisticated, self-aware articulation of exactly the kind of staged epistemic hygiene this rubric rewards.

The paper is remarkably candid about a registered hypothesis being "mostly falsified": the pre-registered prediction of "at least a tenfold difference" between deliberation and serving energy is reported as failing for most configurations ("only batched 1B-class service crosses that threshold"), and the paper reports this without softening or reframing the threshold after the fact. Similarly, the planned I=4000 volatility level is disclosed as having been "amended before any run because it was physically unreachable at this service scale" — an honest, specific, and appropriately-timed (pre-run) protocol amendment rather than a post-hoc rationalization.

Table IV ("What the main observations do and do not establish") is an outstanding and unusual device: it explicitly pairs every major finding with both its "supported reading" and its "unsupported reading" — for example, "19/21 rule wins" supports "calibrated rules are stronger here" but explicitly does *not* support "default rules always suffice," and "14.1 J per decision" supports "control energy is measurable and material" but explicitly does not support "deliberation always dominates serving." This is a remarkably disciplined, proactive guard against exactly the kind of overreading that plagues weaker empirical papers, stated directly in a dedicated table rather than left to be inferred by a careful reader.

The Real-System Check (Section VII) is an excellent, concrete validation practice: rather than resting entirely on the discrete-event emulator, the paper replays three cells against the actual live llama.cpp service and reports the emulator-vs-reality agreement explicitly (within 2%, with every real run "slightly worse" than emulated, correctly noting "the emulator does not inflate performance in these checks" — a directionally conservative bias rather than a flattering one). Combined with the Limitations section's specific, itemized scope constraints (one service, one device, one task family and prompt per arm; hosted model IDs lacking dated snapshots; heavy-tailed top-volatility realization; energy measured in one power mode only; the emulator validated at only three of 21 cells) and its explicit statement that "mechanical traceability checks arithmetic but cannot detect a biased comparison" — directly motivating why the adversarial-audit practice was necessary as a *complement* to reproducibility rather than a substitute for it — this paper's honesty and self-critical rigor are comprehensive and sustained throughout, fully earning the maximum score in this category.

## Category 5: Structure, Clarity, and Formatting
**Score: 14/15**

The paper follows a clear, logically sequenced structure — Introduction (with explicit RQ1–RQ3) → System and Experimental Design → RQ1: Where Does LLM Control Pay? → What the Intent Changes → RQ2: Decision Reproducibility → RQ3: Deliberation Energy → Real-System Check → Discussion → Limitations and Research Process → Disclosure Statement — and the paper's organization around its three explicitly stated research questions gives it a highly traceable argumentative backbone, with each RQ section directly answering the question posed in the Introduction and then further refined through the intent-variant and real-system-check sections that logically follow from and stress-test the RQ1 findings.

The visual presentation is genuinely effective: Fig. 1's heatmap of paired ΔU across the full 3×3×3 volatility-cadence-controller grid, with asterisks marking confidence intervals excluding zero, gives an immediate, comprehensive visual summary of the paper's central finding; Fig. 2's forest-plot-style display of intent-variant effects against their respective comparators is a clear and appropriately labeled way to present many paired comparisons at once; Fig. 3 and Table III's reproducibility results (byte-identical vs. decision-identical rates, broken down by near-threshold/clear-overload/clear-calm state strata) are well-matched to the specific claim about instability concentrating near decision boundaries; and Fig. 4's log-scale energy comparison across serving and deliberation events, with marker size denoting serving slots, is a compact and informative way to convey the RQ3 findings.

Table IV, discussed in Category 4, is also a structural and formatting strength — its supported/unsupported-reading format is a genuinely novel and effective presentational device that most papers, human or AI-authored, do not employ, and it substantially aids reader comprehension of exactly what the paper's dense quantitative findings do and do not license.

The prose is dense and technically demanding, packing many precise quantitative claims into short paragraphs (e.g., the "Incomplete intent" paragraph moves through four distinct numeric claims — slot count change, ΔU magnitude and CI, and a qualitative comparison to the local model's behavior — in three sentences), which, similar to the companion paper by the same authors, imposes real reading effort. This density is a reasonable cost of the paper's genuinely comprehensive scope (21 grid cells, multiple intent variants, two reproducibility studies, and an energy characterization, all within a tight page budget), and the explicit RQ-labeled section headers and Table IV's summary substantially mitigate this cost, but the sheer volume of distinct quantitative claims per paragraph keeps this category just short of a perfect score.

## Category 6: Discussion Potential for the Panel
**Score: 10/10**

This paper is an outstanding case study for the panel, and arguably the single best example in this reviewed pool of a paper whose primary content *is* the demonstration of rigorous adversarial self-correction rather than merely an add-on limitations section. The staged disconfirmation of the LLM's apparent wins — decomposition, multiple-comparison correction, and then a stronger baseline constructed specifically to challenge the result — is a vivid, concrete, and pedagogically ideal artifact for the panel to walk through live: it is a clean, three-step demonstration of exactly how an initially plausible positive finding can and should be interrogated, and it gives the panel a natural, structured basis for a broader discussion about what standard of scrutiny "the Agentic AI Researcher" track should expect before crediting a positive result anywhere in the pool.

Second, this paper's finding directly and substantively engages with a question of immediate relevance to the track's own premise: if calibrated rule-based controllers beat LLM controllers in 19 of 21 tested cells once properly and fairly benchmarked, what does this imply about the broader enthusiasm for LLM-driven autonomous control in networking research (including, implicitly, the track's own interest in agentic AI for network management)? The panel could productively pair this paper with its companion submission (the server-to-edge ranking transfer paper by the same authors) and other LLM-optimistic submissions in this pool to stage a genuinely substantive debate about when and where LLM-based control actually earns its keep versus when it is being compared against under-tuned baselines.

Third, the intent-completeness finding — that removing the cost clause from a natural-language objective causes dramatic, silent over-provisioning while "the prompt remains fluent and valid" — is a specific, vivid, and directly safety-relevant illustration of a distinctive risk of natural-language control interfaces that the panel could use to discuss broader concerns about specification completeness and interpretability in LLM-driven autonomous systems, connecting this paper's narrow empirical finding to a much larger and more consequential question about deploying natural-language-configured controllers in production.

Fourth, the explicit, itemized disclosure of what the fresh-context adversarial-audit agents specifically found (the stronger static baseline, the frozen local controller behavior, the idle-power artifact, and the self-triggered specification gaps) — each of which the panel can independently verify against the paper's own reported results — gives a concrete, checkable case study of genuine self-auditing behavior in an agentic research pipeline, directly comparable to and reinforcing similar practices seen in the strongest papers elsewhere in this pool.

## Summary Recommendation

This paper should be ranked at or very near the top of the top 11 submissions. It delivers a rigorously executed, multi-stage adversarial disconfirmation of its own most flattering finding — showing precisely how an apparent LLM-control advantage over calibrated baselines evaporates once utility is properly decomposed, multiple comparisons are corrected, and a legitimately stronger baseline is constructed — and pairs this central methodological achievement with several additional, independently valuable, mechanism-level findings on intent-completeness risk, hosted-versus-local reproducibility, and controller deliberation energy, each grounded in genuine hardware measurements and validated against a live real-system check. Its results reporting is comprehensively honest, its reference list is small but precisely targeted and evidently carefully curated, and its disclosure statement, while not the most granularly hash-verified in the pool, is specific and substantiated by verifiable in-text evidence of the adversarial-audit practice it describes. Combined with its companion paper's authorship-model discussion value, this submission represents one of the strongest and most methodologically instructive contributions in the entire reviewed batch, and merits serious consideration for a top-tier ranking.
