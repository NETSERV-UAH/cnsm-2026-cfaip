# TPC Review — CNSM 2026 "The Agentic AI Researcher" Track
## Paper: "Adaptive Memory Retention in Diffusion–MARL with Validation for Long-Horizon Decision Making"

---

## Category 1: Technical Correctness, Soundness & Red Flags
**Score: 12/25**

At the level of individual technical building blocks, the paper is competent: the DDPM forward/reverse process (Eq. 1–4) is a correct, standard restatement of Ho et al.'s denoising diffusion formulation, the cost-guided sampling perturbation (Eq. 5–6) is a reasonable application of classifier-guidance-style trajectory optimization, the Dec-POMDP formalization (⟨D,S,A,T,O,O,R,H⟩) is correctly specified, and the CTDE/MAPPO training description (centralized value function discarded at execution, clipped surrogate objective) is textbook-accurate. Individually, none of these components contain a mathematical error.

However, the paper contains a genuinely serious red flag that goes beyond ordinary technical soundness: the **Index Terms field literally reads "component, formatting, style, styling, insert"** — this is the unedited IEEE conference LaTeX template placeholder text, left completely unmodified in the final submission. This is not a subtle error; it is direct, unambiguous evidence that neither the generating system nor any human reviewer read the rendered output closely enough to notice a glaring template artifact sitting in the paper's metadata. A second, related artifact appears mid-methodology: the sentence "*In the next section we will discuss some results.*" is left dangling at the end of Section II-C, an informal, first-draft-style transition sentence that has no business surviving into a final camera-ready submission. Together these are strong, concrete evidence of insufficient editorial review — exactly the kind of red flag this category asks reviewers to hunt for.

On the reference list: most entries are real and verifiable — Ho et al. (DDPM, NeurIPS 2020), Janner et al. (Diffusion planning, ICML 2022), Lowe et al. (MADDPG, NeurIPS 2017), Rashid et al. (QMIX, JMLR 2020), Yu et al. (MAPPO, NeurIPS 2022), Schaul et al. (PER, arXiv 2015), and Van den Berg et al. (RVO, ICRA 2008) are all legitimate, well-known, correctly attributed works. However, several entries warrant scrutiny the panel should perform independently: reference [6] (Liang, Koenig, Fioretto, AAAI 2026, vol. 40, no. 28, pp. 23417–23424) cites a 2026 AAAI proceeding with an unusually specific page range for a conference that had not yet occurred as of most training cutoffs — plausible given web-search-augmented generation, but unverifiable by this reviewer and worth spot-checking. Reference [28] ("SyncTwin," arXiv:2601.09920, 2026) uses an arXiv identifier format (2601.xxxxx) implying January 2026, again at the edge of verifiability. Neither is necessarily fabricated, but their recency and specificity place them in the same risk category the track explicitly asks reviewers to flag.

The deeper soundness problem is not any single equation but the **Table III comparison itself**: CMR-FDT is reported as simultaneously best on every single metric — lowest collision rate (1.2% vs. next-best 4.8%), highest success (97.8% vs. 91.2%), lowest memory (2.3G vs. next-best 8.7G), and highest stability (96.1% vs. 71.2%) — against five different baselines spanning classical (A*+RVO), MARL-only (MADDPG, MAPPO), and diffusion-only (DiffPolicy, DecDiffuser) approaches, with no variance, no confidence intervals, and no seed count reported across any table. A method that dominates every axis against every category of baseline simultaneously, with no reported statistical uncertainty anywhere in the paper, is a strong indicator of the "magical outcomes" failure mode this rubric is specifically designed to catch, and it substantially undercuts confidence in the technical soundness of the results, independent of whether the underlying architecture description is coherent.

## Category 2: Novelty, Research Value & Core Strengths
**Score: 10/20**

The conceptual pitch — fusing diffusion-based trajectory refinement with MARL-based coordination, then adding a stability-aware memory retention mechanism that feeds obsolescence signals back into training, all wrapped in a digital-twin pre-deployment gate — is a reasonably creative combination of three active research threads (generative trajectory planning, multi-agent coordination, and lifelong/selective experience replay) that are not commonly integrated in exactly this three-way configuration in the cited prior work. The paper correctly identifies that MADiff and DoF (its two closest cited comparators) operate offline and lack both online refinement and memory governance, which is a legitimate differentiation if accurate.

The most conceptually interesting individual component is the stability score ψn (Eq. 8), an EMA-style decay-weighted normalization of coordination outcome that determines pruning eligibility — this is a sensible mechanism, distinct from vanilla FIFO or TD-error-prioritized replay (PER), and the explicit contrast the paper draws against Schaul et al.'s PER (prioritizing by TD-error magnitude rather than "coordination-level stability or inter-agent usage frequency") shows real awareness of the relevant literature's mechanism, not just its existence.

However, the paper's claimed research value is difficult to credit given the concerns raised in Category 1. A contribution's novelty is only as convincing as the evidence supporting it, and here the "97.8% navigation success, 87.5% memory reduction, 96.1% stability" headline results — repeated verbatim in the Abstract, Introduction, and Conclusion without any caveat — are the paper's only support for the claim that this fusion actually works better than the alternatives. Absent any reported variance, ablation significance testing, or qualitative trace analysis of a specific failure/success case (contrast this with, e.g., a paper that shows a concrete incident trace), the reader cannot distinguish a genuine architectural advance from an internally-consistent but unverified set of generated numbers. The idea is worth pursuing; the paper as written does not yet earn the "advances the state of the art" claim it makes.

Relative to other AI-authored submissions in this pool that explicitly disclosed "no experimental results claimed" when no real experiment was run, this paper's choice to present detailed, precise quantitative tables without any hedging is a less honest research posture, which further discounts its net research value regardless of how creative the underlying architectural idea might be.

## Category 3: Adherence to Track Rules & Disclosure
**Score: 8/15**

The mandatory LLM Use Disclosure is present and identifies two specific models (OpenAI GPT-5.6 and Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.6) used across literature search, manuscript drafting, technical reasoning, "experimental and code assistance," result interpretation, and diagram generation. This satisfies the basic disclosure requirement. However, this paper differs fundamentally from the other submissions reviewed in this batch in a way the panel must weigh carefully: it is attributed to three named human authors with real institutional affiliations (SRM Institute, Ericsson Research India), and the disclosure explicitly states that "the authors retained responsibility for the research hypothesis, framework design, methodological decisions, experimental execution and validation, result interpretation, citation verification, and final manuscript" and that "all generated content... were reviewed and validated by the authors before inclusion."

This framing places the paper in tension with the track's premise of evaluating papers "100% generated by an AI." If the disclosure is taken at face value, this is a conventionally-authored paper that used LLMs as a drafting and coding aid, with the named authors bearing responsibility for the (apparently fabricated-looking) experimental claims — which would make the Category 1 and 2 concerns a matter of *author* diligence rather than *AI* limitation, and arguably outside the track's intended evaluation frame entirely. Alternatively, if — as the leftover "component, formatting, style, styling, insert" template text and the dangling "we will discuss some results" sentence strongly suggest — the claimed human validation did not actually occur with genuine rigor, then the disclosure statement itself is not fully accurate, which is a more serious integrity concern than anything in the other three papers reviewed.

Either reading is problematic for this category. If the human-validation claim is accurate, the paper may not belong in an "AI Research Agent" evaluation track at all. If the human-validation claim is not accurate in practice — which the surviving template artifacts strongly suggest — then the disclosure overstates the rigor actually applied, undermining exactly the transparency this category is meant to reward. The AI tone itself is reasonably consistent through the technical sections, but the unedited placeholder text and the informal dangling sentence are themselves evidence against the claim of thorough human review, which is the crux of what this category is supposed to verify.

## Category 4: Results, Evaluation, and Honesty on Limitations
**Score: 3/15**

This is the paper's weakest category by a wide margin. As noted above, every comparison table (I, II, III) shows the proposed method winning every single metric against every single baseline, with precise-looking percentages (97.8%, 87.5%, 96.1%, 89.8%, 96.9%, 98.2%, etc.) reported to one decimal place but with zero variance, zero confidence intervals, and no stated number of random seeds or repeated trials anywhere in the text — despite the paper describing a specific, seemingly real experimental setup (24 robots, 120m×120m region, 85 static + 37 dynamic obstacles, 20Hz updates, three named datasets/benchmarks). This combination — highly specific experimental parameters paired with suspiciously uniform, unhedged, always-best results — is a textbook example of the "magical outcomes" failure mode this rubric explicitly warns against, and it is the single most damaging feature of this paper against this criterion.

Compounding this, **there is no Limitations section at all.** Every other paper reviewed in this batch — including the three that ran real, honestly-reported experiments — devoted a full, substantive section to caveating scope, generalizability, and known weaknesses of their own method. This paper's Conclusion-equivalent material (the final "Inference" paragraph under Section III-F) is purely self-congratulatory ("each component addressing a specific limitation that no single existing method solves"), with no acknowledgment that a Dec-POMDP formalization, a diffusion trajectory refiner, a stability-weighted memory buffer, and a digital-twin certification gate — four non-trivial subsystems — might individually or jointly introduce failure modes, computational overhead, hyperparameter sensitivity (λ1, λ2, λ3, η, ψmin, δdeploy are all introduced but never given values or a sensitivity analysis), or sim-to-real gaps beyond the single reported "96.9% sim-to-real success" figure.

The "Inference" prose interspersed after each table (e.g., "proving that memory intelligence actively improves coordination rather than passively storing data") uses causal, confident language disproportionate to what a single-run comparison table without statistical testing can actually establish. A methodologically careful paper would frame such a claim as "consistent with" rather than "proving," and the repeated use of this overconfident register throughout Section III is itself a pattern worth flagging: it is the opposite of the calibrated, hedge-appropriate honesty that the strongest papers in this pool demonstrate.

## Category 5: Structure, Clarity, and Formatting
**Score: 9/15**

Structurally, the paper follows a recognizable IEEE conference shape — Abstract → Introduction → Methodology (three subsections: diffusion refinement, MARL coordination, memory retention + digital twin) → Experimental Evaluation (six lettered subsections walking through the pipeline stage by stage) → References — and the six-subsection experimental breakdown (III-A through III-F) does mirror the four-component architecture in a logically traceable way, which aids navigability.

The mathematical notation is mostly used correctly and consistently (τ, s_h, α_bar_t, ψ_n, ξ_n recur with stable meaning across sections), and Figure captions and inference paragraphs are tightly coupled to the tables they describe, which is good practice when it works. However, formatting execution is noticeably weaker than the other papers reviewed in this batch: beyond the Index Terms and dangling-sentence issues already flagged, Figure 2's diagram text contains an apparent duplication ("Remove congestion and path conflicts / Evaluate effect on neighbouring agents" appears twice in the extracted figure content under both "Interaction-aware reasoning" and "Trajectory retention"), suggesting the diagram itself was generated with a copy-paste artifact that was not caught before submission.

The prose in the "Inference" paragraphs is also stylistically repetitive and promotional in a way that reduces clarity — near-identical sentence templates ("CMR-FDT achieves X% while retaining Y%, proving/validating Z") recur across Sections III-B through III-F, giving the results section a marketing-copy cadence rather than an analytical one. This is a real clarity cost: a reader looking for the actual experimental protocol (how many trials, what statistical test, what hardware) has to search hard for information that isn't there, because the space is instead filled with restated superlatives. Combined with the leftover template artifacts, the overall impression is of a paper that was assembled quickly and not proofread carefully before submission, despite the disclosure's claim of thorough human review.

## Category 6: Discussion Potential for the Panel
**Score: 8/10**

This paper is an excellent — arguably the single most valuable — case study in this pool for exposing a real AI-research-pipeline failure mode that the panel should discuss explicitly: the survival of unedited template placeholder text ("component, formatting, style, styling, insert") directly into a submitted paper, alongside a dangling first-draft sentence ("In the next section we will discuss some results"), is concrete, undeniable evidence of a human/AI review process that did not actually catch basic hygiene errors, despite a disclosure statement explicitly claiming rigorous human validation of "all generated content." This is a much sharper and more concrete discussion prompt than an abstract debate about disclosure norms — it's a smoking gun the panel can point to directly.

Second, the tension between this paper's claimed authorship model (named human authors retaining "responsibility for... experimental execution and validation") and its observable characteristics (uniformly-winning, variance-free, unhedged results with no Limitations section) gives the panel a genuinely productive question to debate: what should a review track do when a disclosure statement's claims about human oversight appear inconsistent with the artifact's actual evidence of oversight? This is a different and arguably more important question than anything raised by the fully-autonomous submissions in this pool, which at least did not claim a level of human validation their artifacts don't support.

Third, the "every metric wins by a wide margin against every baseline category" pattern in Table III is a clean, teachable example of the "too good to be true" heuristic that reviewers (human or automated) should learn to distrust on sight, and the panel could usefully contrast this paper's results section against the null-operator paper's genuinely mixed, regime-dependent findings in the same pool to illustrate what honest versus suspicious result reporting looks like side by side.

The one factor moderating this category's score is that the paper's discussion value is almost entirely *cautionary* rather than demonstrating impressive capability — it is a strong example of what to watch out for, not a strong example of agentic research capability, which slightly narrows its usefulness if the panel wants a balanced mix of positive and negative case studies rather than a primarily negative one.

## Summary Recommendation

This paper should **not** be ranked among the top 11 submissions. While it proposes a conceptually reasonable fusion of diffusion-based trajectory refinement, MARL coordination, stability-aware memory retention, and digital-twin pre-deployment validation, the artifact itself shows clear, concrete evidence of insufficient review: unedited IEEE template placeholder text left in the Index Terms field, a dangling draft sentence in the methodology, an apparent duplicated diagram fragment, and — most seriously — a full results section in which the proposed method wins every single metric against every single baseline with no variance, no confidence intervals, and no Limitations section anywhere in the paper. This pattern is a textbook example of the "magical," over-confident outcome presentation this rubric explicitly warns against, and it stands in stark contrast to the more honest, appropriately-hedged reporting seen in the stronger submissions in this pool. The disclosure statement's claim that human authors rigorously validated all experimental execution and results sits uncomfortably alongside this evidence, and the panel should treat this paper as a valuable cautionary case study precisely because of that tension — but not as a candidate for the top tier of submissions.
