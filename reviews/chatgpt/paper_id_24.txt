## Category 1: Technical Correctness, Soundness & Red Flags

**Score: 17/25**

The core control formulation is coherent and technically plausible as a **proof-of-concept management loop**. IRIS represents an operator intent as a QoE threshold plus a slice-priority budget, observes delay and loss-event telemetry, evaluates candidate codec/network actions through an analytical QoE model, and chooses the minimum-bitrate action predicted to satisfy the intent.  The estimator is dimensionally sensible: the MOS mapping remains in the nominal 1–5 range, the queue term grows with utilization, and \(\hat\lambda A_c/F\) is dimensionless. The distinction between frame-independent Draco and inter-frame V-PCC is also directionally reasonable. The cited V-PCC overview, immersive-delivery tutorial, QUIC RFC, and PCQM paper are all genuine and broadly support the background discussion. ([Cambridge University Press][1])

The largest technical red flag is that **the controller's analytical model and the simulator's “ground truth” are nearly the same model**. Realized QoE uses the same functional form, intrinsic qualities, amplification factors, queueing relation, and latency sensitivity as the IRIS estimator; the principal difference is that loss becomes stochastic.   As a result, the model-inversion controller has privileged knowledge of the world that generated its evaluation. The later miscalibration experiment perturbs only \(\hat\alpha\), while preserving the exact structural form, \(A_c\), queueing law, priority effect, and telemetry relationships. This makes the 32× off-design robustness result much closer to **correct-model control under parameter perturbation** than evidence that the approach would remain robust to genuine model mismatch.

Several domain assumptions also need stronger qualification. Reliable QUIC retransmits lost data, but converting a loss event into an \(A_c\)-frame stall of one RTT is a very coarse abstraction: the consequences depend on packetization, stream mapping, reference-frame structure, congestion control, retransmission timing, and decoder buffering. Likewise, the slice-priority action simply multiplies delay by 0.6 and loss rate by 0.5 without modeling scheduler contention, resource reservation, or impact on other users.  These are acceptable simulation assumptions when explicitly labeled—as the authors do—but they prevent the results from establishing real private-5G behavior. I found **no clear fabricated reference or fake DOI** in the ten-item bibliography; however, the literature review misses directly adjacent work such as the 2024 IFIP Networking paper *Enabling User Intent-based Network Path Adaptation for Live Volumetric Streaming*, which already combines user intent, volumetric streaming, edge adaptation, and measured QoE. ([University of Bristol][2]) This weakens the paper's “to our knowledge” positioning.

## Category 2: Novelty, Research Value & Core Strengths

**Score: 14/20**

The paper's strongest idea is the **codec-aware inversion of an operator QoE intent**, rather than conventional bitrate maximization or generic threshold adaptation. The controller reasons over both application-level codec structure and network-level telemetry, then actuates both codec configuration and slice priority.  This cross-layer management formulation is genuinely relevant to CNSM: it takes a vague business/operator objective (“MOS at least 3.5 with limited priority use”) and turns it into a closed-loop decision rule. The distinction between maximizing MOS and satisfying an intent at minimum resource cost is particularly valuable—the lower mean MOS of IRIS is correctly interpreted as returned resource headroom rather than worse management. 

The novelty is nevertheless **integration novelty rather than a new control principle**. Model inversion, QoE-aware adaptation, point-cloud streaming adaptation, intent-driven network control, and cross-layer radio/content/resource optimization all have substantial prior art. In particular, Qian et al. already reported intent-based network path adaptation for live volumetric streaming with real experiments in 2024, and a 2026 O-RAN study jointly optimizes radio, compute, and volumetric-content variables under a QoE model. ([Biblioteca Digital Abierta IFIP][3]) IRIS's narrower combination—reliable-QUIC loss telemetry, explicit codec amplification, and zero-touch MOS-intent enforcement—may still be distinctive, but the Related Work section is too small to demonstrate that boundary rigorously.

The research value is therefore strongest in the **management hypothesis** it exposes: a model-aware policy may generalize across operating regimes better than fixed telemetry thresholds because it recomputes the feasibility of each action instead of memorizing cutoffs. That is a useful and falsifiable idea. The miscalibration study is also a good instinct because it asks where analytical inversion stops working rather than showing only nominal gains.  A significantly stronger contribution would test that principle with a ground-truth model not shared by the controller, or with traces/packet-level emulation, and would include several model-aware and tuned rule-based comparators so that the benefit can be attributed specifically to inversion rather than to privileged structural knowledge.

## Category 3: Adherence to Track Rules & Disclosure

**Score: 13/15**

Formally, the submission is very strong. It is exactly five pages, follows IEEE two-column formatting, is clearly within the allowed 5G/6G, intent-based, and zero-touch management topics, and includes an extensive mandatory Disclosure Statement. The CFP permits the Research Director to select and constrain the topic but requires all technical content to be AI-generated, as well as disclosure of the model, exact initial prompting strategy, experiment framework, and constraints. ([CNSM Conference][4]) The paper identifies Claude Fable 5 as the sole research model, and Fable 5 is a genuine Anthropic model available through Claude.ai in July 2026. ([Anthropic][5])

The disclosure is among the most complete in the set. It reproduces the **entire initial prompt verbatim**, states that no further technical instructions were supplied, gives Python/NumPy versions and seed ranges, identifies the sandbox execution environment, explains figure generation and IEEEtran compilation, documents live reference verification, and explicitly distinguishes automated from human-supplied components.   I see no obvious evidence of subsequently inserted human-written technical prose. The model's voice and terminology remain consistent throughout the technical core.

The reason I do not award full marks is that the **human initial prompt is unusually technically prescriptive**. It supplies not only background but an almost complete candidate research direction: QoE-driven intent enforcement for volumetric XR over private 5G, an analytical QoE estimator consuming latency/loss/throughput, codec/slice/placement actuation, simulation against static and threshold baselines, and intent-violation/resource-efficiency metrics.  It even provides several domain assertions that the final paper directly adopts. The CFP permits humans to constrain the initial prompt, so this is not an explicit violation, but it makes the paper a weaker test of autonomous **problem formulation** than submissions where the AI independently selected the technical approach from a much broader topic prompt.

## Category 4: Results, Evaluation, and Honesty on Limitations

**Score: 10/15**

The reported numbers are internally coherent and the evaluation has several strengths: 30 seeds, 600-second sessions, a stochastic semi-Markov network state process, noisy telemetry, explicit model–reality stochasticity, static and reactive baselines, a severity sweep, and an estimator-miscalibration experiment.  Table II reports not only violation rate but MOS, bandwidth, priority use, and switching frequency, which appropriately exposes the resource/safety trade-off. The paper also preserves an unfavorable point: at \(\lambda_d=2/s\), IRIS and the threshold policy are statistically indistinguishable.  That is good scientific behavior.

The main evaluation weakness is **baseline fairness**. The supposedly “well-tuned” threshold policy is presented as a single hand-specified mapping, but the paper never explains a tuning search, held-out calibration procedure, objective function, or parameter sweep.  More importantly, its low-loss action is V-PCC-H at 45 Mb/s, even though V-PCC-L at 20 Mb/s is available and is precisely the kind of configuration that drives IRIS's bandwidth advantage. No low-bandwidth static baseline or exhaustively optimized threshold controller is included. Consequently, the headline “51% less bandwidth” may reflect not only codec-aware inversion but also a comparator that was never optimized for bandwidth under the same intent. The central 1.06% versus 1.49% violation comparison also lacks a paired confidence interval or hypothesis test for the *difference*, despite the paper's claim that C1 is supported.

The limitations section is unusually candid and materially improves the paper. It explicitly states that the MOS model coefficients were **not fitted to subjective data**, that the V-PCC amplification values are schematic, that queueing is simplified, that no physical 5G testbed or human raters exist, and that the absolute MOS/violation values have no external validity.   However, it does not fully confront the most important threat: IRIS is evaluated inside a world whose QoE law is essentially the same law IRIS inverts. That structural circularity, plus the weak threshold baseline, means the impressive off-design result should be interpreted as a **simulation sanity demonstration of model-based control**, not yet robust evidence that IRIS outperforms strong alternatives in realistic networks.

## Category 5: Structure, Clarity, and Formatting

**Score: 14/15**

The paper is very well structured. The Abstract states the management problem, falsifiable claims, simulation nature, and headline numbers without pretending to have a physical deployment.  The Introduction clearly distinguishes the management contribution from pure media-quality modeling, while the method progresses logically from operator intent through telemetry, QoE estimation, candidate actions, and minimum-cost selection. Figure 1 on page 2 is particularly effective: it makes the scope boundary explicit by separating the managed reliable-QUIC private-5G path from the transparent HMD display hop. 

The Results section is also well narrated. Figure 2 gives a representative state-transition trace and shows why V-PCC becomes undesirable as loss severity rises, while Figure 3 on page 4 tests both off-design severity and estimator miscalibration rather than merely displaying nominal averages.   The prose makes several useful distinctions: intent satisfaction is not MOS maximization; local fixed rules can be competitive near their design point; and model inversion carries a calibration burden. These are concise and technically meaningful explanations.

The one presentation deduction is that some model assumptions are expressed with a precision that could suggest stronger empirical grounding than actually exists. Quantities such as \(Q_c=0.86\), \(A_c=16\), \(\alpha=8\,s^{-1}\), the slice multipliers 0.6/0.5, and the MOS threshold 3.5 are all cleanly typeset but essentially assumed.  The paper does say this explicitly later, so this is not deception; nevertheless, a compact table labeling each parameter as **measured / sourced / assumed** would make the epistemic status clearer. Otherwise, the five-page artifact is polished, readable, and professionally formatted.

## Category 6: Discussion Potential for the Panel

**Score: 10/10**

This is an excellent panel artifact because it demonstrates a capable autonomous researcher constructing a **coherent cross-domain systems hypothesis** from networking, transport, media coding, QoE, and intent-based management. The AI designed and ran a stochastic control experiment, generated a formal estimator and controller, created useful figures, performed sensitivity analysis, compiled to exactly five pages, and verified a compact bibliography. The disclosure is particularly valuable because the entire prompt is visible, letting the panel examine exactly how much of that apparent autonomy came from the model versus the Research Directors' detailed technical grounding. 

It also exposes a sophisticated AI-science failure mode: **building the world to fit the model**. IRIS's internal estimator and simulation ground truth share essentially the same analytical structure, while the principal baseline does not receive equivalent codec knowledge. The resulting off-design superiority is therefore plausible but partly engineered into the experiment. This is not fabricated data; it is more subtle. An autonomous system can design a logically consistent, reproducible experiment whose assumptions structurally privilege its own proposed mechanism. That is exactly the kind of issue a human expert may detect even when every equation and number is internally correct.

The paper further raises an important governance question about the track itself. The human prompt explicitly supplies the volumetric-video/codec/QUIC interpretation and proposes almost the same closed-loop management architecture eventually implemented by the AI.  The AI's execution is impressive, but the panel can productively debate where **topic constraint ends and technical co-design begins**. Combined with the clean references, candid limitations, missed adjacent literature, and baseline-design issue, IRIS is a richer panel case than a simple citation-hallucination paper because the central questions concern experimental epistemology and autonomy boundaries.

# FINAL SCORE: **78/100**

**Top-11 recommendation:** **No—borderline.** I would place IRIS just outside the top 11 on scientific merit. It is relevant, polished, citation-clean, highly compliant, explicitly falsifiable, and much more honest than many synthetic studies. Its core idea—intent-driven codec-aware QoE model inversion—is useful and potentially publishable after stronger validation. However, the current evaluation is too self-confirming: the controller effectively inverts the same QoE law that generates ground truth, only one parameter is meaningfully miscalibrated, the threshold comparator is not demonstrably “well tuned” and omits an obvious low-bandwidth action in its good-state branch, and directly adjacent intent-based volumetric-streaming literature is missing. Those limitations keep it below the strongest experimentally adversarial submissions, though it remains an excellent panel artifact.
